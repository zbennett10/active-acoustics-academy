# The parts of loss

## What transmission loss means

**Transmission loss**, TL, is how much weaker the sound is at a field point than it was one meter from the source:

$$
TL = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{I_{1\,\text{m}}}{I_{\text{field}}}\right)
$$

It is a positive number of decibels, it grows with range in general, and it is a property of the **path**: the same source depth, receiver depth, frequency and ocean give the same loss whatever the source level.

Loss is **one way**. An active sonar pays it twice, once out to the target and once back. In a monostatic geometry the two are the same number, so the echo carries $2 \times TL$. An error of 3 dB in transmission loss is an error of 6 dB in the echo.

A principle called **reciprocity** says loss is unchanged if source and receiver swap places. The loss from a sensor at 50 m to a target at 300 m equals the loss from 300 m back to 50 m. This is why one engine run from the sensor serves both directions.

:::pipeline
Any product labeled as loss should say whether it is one way or two way. Both are in use, both are called TL in conversation, and they differ by a factor of two in decibels.
:::

## Spreading

Most loss at short range is geometry. The same power is spread over a growing area.

**Spherical spreading.** In open water sound expands as a sphere whose area goes as range squared:

$$
TL = 20 \log_{10} r
$$

That is 60 dB at 1 km and 6 dB more for each doubling of range.

**Cylindrical spreading.** Once sound is confined between surface and bottom, or inside a duct, it can only spread sideways. Area goes as range, not range squared:

$$
TL = 10 \log_{10} r
$$

only 3 dB per doubling.

Real paths begin spherical and become cylindrical once the sound has filled the channel, at a **transition range** $r_t$ comparable to the channel depth:

$$
TL = 20 \log_{10} r_t + 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{r}{r_t}\right) \qquad (r > r_t)
$$

When nothing better is known, a middle course of $15 \log r$ is used and called practical spreading.

| Range | Spherical | Cylindrical beyond 1 km |
|---|---|---|
| 1 km | 60 dB | 60 dB |
| 10 km | 80 dB | 70 dB |
| 100 km | 100 dB | 80 dB |

:::key
Spreading laws are sanity checks, not predictions. If an engine reports far less than cylindrical loss, or far more than spherical plus absorption in open water, something is wrong.
:::

## Absorption

Seawater turns a little of the sound into heat. The loss is proportional to distance, so it is written as a coefficient $\alpha$ in decibels per kilometer and added to spreading:

$$
TL = \text{spreading} + \alpha\, r
$$

Absorption rises steeply with frequency. Dissolved boric acid dominates below a few kilohertz and magnesium sulfate above, with pure water taking over at very high frequency. Thorp’s formula, with $f$ in kilohertz, is the usual quick estimate:

$$
\alpha = \frac{0.11 f^2}{1 + f^2} + \frac{44 f^2}{4100 + f^2} + 2.75 \times 10^{-4} f^2 + 0.003\ \ \text{dB/km}
$$

| Frequency | α | Loss over 50 km, one way |
|---|---|---|
| 300 Hz | 0.01 dB/km | 0.6 dB |
| 1 kHz | 0.07 dB/km | 3.5 dB |
| 3.5 kHz | 0.24 dB/km | 12 dB |
| 10 kHz | 1.2 dB/km | 59 dB |

This table explains the shape of the whole field. Long-range sonars work at low frequency because nothing else survives the trip, and an active sonar pays the absorption twice.

:::pipeline
Absorption also depends on temperature, salinity, depth and acidity. Engines differ in which formula they use. Two engines given the same ocean can differ by a few decibels at long range for this reason alone.
:::

## Boundary losses

Every time a path touches the surface or the seabed it gives something up.

- **Surface loss** per bounce grows with wind and frequency. On a calm day at low frequency it is close to nothing. At several kilohertz in a rough sea it can be a few decibels per bounce.
- **Bottom loss** per bounce depends on sediment and grazing angle, as described in unit 3: small below the critical angle, large above it.

The losses add up along the path. A ray that bounces off the bottom twenty times at half a decibel each has lost 10 dB to the bottom. A ray that bounces six times at 8 dB each is gone.

That arithmetic sorts the paths. Over distance, steep rays that bounce often and lose much per bounce are stripped away, and the energy that remains travels at shallow angles. This is why loss in shallow water starts cylindrical and then grows faster, and why the vertical spread of arriving sound narrows with range.

:::operator
In shallow water with a soft bottom, range is short and nothing about the sonar will change that much. In shallow water with a hard bottom and a calm surface, ranges can surprise you. The bottom chart tells you which day you are having.
:::

# Reading the field

## The full-field plot

A **full-field** plot shows transmission loss at every range and depth for one source depth, along one bearing. Range runs across, depth runs down, and color shows loss. It is the single most informative picture in propagation.

::play{preset="surface-duct"}

Learn to find these features:

- **The source.** Loss is least beside it, on the left edge at the source depth.
- **Ducts.** Horizontal bands of low loss that persist with range.
- **Shadow zones.** Dark regions where little energy arrives. Their edges can be sharp.
- **Convergence zones.** Bright patches near the surface far from the source, connected to the source by a deep arc.
- **Bottom-bounce patterns.** Repeating diagonal stripes between surface and seabed.

:::try
Drag the dashed receiver line from inside the duct to just below it and watch the loss curve underneath jump by 15 dB or more. Then turn on the vertical transmit beam, steer it a few degrees down and see which part of the field it lights.
:::

:::pipeline
A full-field is a two-dimensional array, range by depth, for one sensor, one frequency or band and one bearing. A complete result has one of these for every bearing radial.
:::

## Loss curves and what they hide

Slicing a full-field at one depth gives a **loss curve**: transmission loss against range for a receiver, or a target, at that depth. Loss curves are what the sonar equation consumes. They are also where information gets thrown away.

A curve is true for one depth. In a strongly layered ocean, the curves for a target at 50 m and at 150 m can differ by 20 dB over most of their length. A prediction made for one target depth says little about another, and the target chooses its depth.

Reciprocity gives a useful reading of the same plot. A full-field computed from a sensor at 50 m shows the loss to a target at any depth. By reciprocity it equally shows the loss to the sensor at 50 m from a source at any depth.

:::operator
“Best depth” questions are answered on the full-field, not the loss curve. To deny a target a hiding place, look for the depth band where the field is darkest and ask what would light it: a different sensor depth, usually.
:::

## Coherent, incoherent and band-averaged

Sound reaching a point by several paths arrives as several waves. They can be combined in two ways.

**Coherently**, respecting phase. Waves that arrive in step reinforce and waves out of step cancel. The result has fine ripples in range and depth, with swings of 10 dB or more over a few wavelengths.

**Incoherently**, adding powers and ignoring phase. The ripples disappear and a smooth average remains.

Which is right? For a single pure tone and a perfectly known ocean, the coherent answer is the true one. For prediction, the fine ripples are not knowable: they shift with a meter of depth or a fraction of a percent of sound speed. Processors also work over a band, and averaging across a band smears the ripples out anyway.

- **Incoherent** or **band-averaged** loss is the usual choice for performance prediction.
- **Coherent** loss is needed at low frequencies, near the surface, and wherever the interference itself is the effect being studied.

:::pitfall
A coherent field sampled coarsely looks like noise, and two runs with slightly different grids appear to disagree by 10 dB cell by cell. Before chasing that as a bug, check whether the engine was asked for coherent output.
:::

# Engines

## Ray and Gaussian-beam engines

A **ray engine** launches a fan of rays from the source and follows each one through the sound speed field, applying Snell’s law at every step and a loss at every boundary. Intensity comes from how the rays spread apart.

Rays are a high-frequency approximation. They work when the wavelength is small compared with the water depth and the features of the profile. For active sonar frequencies of a kilohertz and above in most water, they are the natural tool.

Classical rays have two flaws. They predict infinite intensity where rays cross, at **caustics**, and exactly zero in shadow zones, where real sound leaks in by diffraction. **Gaussian-beam** methods repair both by giving every ray a finite width, so each one contributes a little energy to its surroundings. BELLHOP uses Gaussian beams. GRAB, the Gaussian Ray Bundle engine inside the Navy’s CASS model, uses a closely related idea.

The great advantage for active sonar is that rays give **eigenrays**: each path to a point, with its own launch angle, arrival angle, travel time and loss. Reverberation, beam patterns and target strength all need exactly that.

:::pipeline
The playgrounds on this site run a small ray engine of this kind in your browser: a few hundred to a few thousand rays, each carrying a Gaussian slice of energy. It is a teaching model, and it follows the same logic as the real ones.
:::

## Parabolic equation engines

A **parabolic equation** engine solves the wave equation directly. It starts with the field at the source and marches it outward in range, one step at a time, updating the whole water column at each step.

Because it is a wave solution, it includes diffraction, leakage out of ducts and interference without special treatment. It handles ocean and bathymetry that change with range naturally, since each step can use new properties. This makes it the standard choice at **low frequency** and in complicated environments. RAM is the widely used research code. NSPE, the Navy Standard Parabolic Equation, fills the same role in the Navy’s standard set.

Its costs are the mirror image of a ray engine’s strengths:

- Work grows with frequency, since the grid must resolve the wavelength. Kilohertz runs over long ranges become expensive.
- The output is a field of pressure, not a list of paths. Arrival angles and travel times are not given directly and have to be extracted when needed.

:::key
A rough division of labor: parabolic equation engines below about a kilohertz and where the water is complex, ray engines above that. Systems that carry both choose by frequency.
:::

## Normal modes and wavenumber integration

Two more families complete the picture.

**Normal modes** treat the ocean as a waveguide, like a string fixed at both ends. The field is a sum of standing-wave shapes in depth, the modes, each travelling outward at its own speed and decaying at its own rate. Modes are efficient in shallow water at low frequency, where only a handful exist, and they explain mode stripping and cutoff cleanly. KRAKEN is the best-known code. Range dependence is handled approximately, by letting modes adapt slowly or by coupling them.

**Wavenumber integration** computes the exact solution for a horizontally layered ocean. It is the reference against which others are checked. OASES and SCOOTER are examples. It cannot handle range dependence at all.

| Engine family | Best at | Weak at | Gives angles and times |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ray, Gaussian beam | High frequency, any range dependence | Low frequency, diffraction | Yes |
| Parabolic equation | Low frequency, range dependence | High frequency cost | Not directly |
| Normal modes | Low frequency, shallow, slowly varying | Strong range dependence | Indirectly |
| Wavenumber integration | Exact benchmarks | Any range dependence | No |

## Choosing and trusting an engine

No engine is right everywhere. A few habits make any of them trustworthy.

- **Match the engine to the frequency.** Rays below a few hundred hertz in shallow water will be wrong. A parabolic equation at 10 kHz over 100 km will be slow.
- **Know the standard set.** The Navy maintains approved models and databases in the Oceanographic and Atmospheric Master Library, OAML. CASS with GRAB and NSPE are members. Approved does not mean valid outside its stated frequency range.
- **Compare against a simple law.** Loss should sit between cylindrical and spherical plus absorption, except in shadows.
- **Compare engines with each other** where their ranges of validity overlap.
- **Remember the inputs.** In practice, the error from an uncertain bottom or a stale profile is usually larger than the difference between two good engines.

A newer option is the **surrogate**: a machine-learned model trained on many engine runs that returns a loss field in milliseconds. Recent work uses neural operators and physics-guided probabilistic models. They are promising for speed and for ensembles, and they inherit every limitation of the engine and the environments they were trained on.

:::operator
When a prediction looks wrong, the order of suspicion is: the inputs, then the plumbing between the stages, then the engine. The engine is usually the most carefully checked part of the chain.
:::
