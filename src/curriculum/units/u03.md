# Sound speed

## What sets the speed of sound

Sound travels through seawater at about 1500 meters per second, more than four times its speed in air. The exact value changes by a few percent from place to place, and those few percent control where sound goes.

Three things set it:

- **Temperature.** Warmer water is faster. Near 10 °C, one degree adds roughly 3.5 to 4 m/s. This is the strongest effect in the upper ocean.
- **Pressure**, which means depth. Each 100 m of depth adds about 1.6 m/s. Below the reach of surface heating this is the only thing still changing.
- **Salinity.** Each part per thousand adds about 1.3 m/s. It matters near rivers, ice and fronts, and little elsewhere.

A compact formula that captures all three, with $T$ in degrees Celsius, $S$ in parts per thousand and $z$ in meters:

$$
c \approx 1449.2 + 4.6T - 0.055T^2 + 0.00029T^3 + (1.34 - 0.010T)(S - 35) + 0.016z
$$

:::key
In the upper ocean temperature wins, so sound speed falls as the water cools with depth. In the deep ocean pressure wins, so sound speed rises again. That turnaround creates most of the structure in this unit.
:::

## The sound speed profile

A **sound speed profile** is sound speed plotted against depth. Reading one is the first skill of acoustic prediction. A typical open-ocean profile has four parts.

- **Mixed layer.** Wind stirs the top tens of meters to one temperature. With temperature constant, pressure raises sound speed slightly with depth. The bottom of this layer is the **sonic layer depth**.
- **Thermocline.** Temperature falls quickly, and sound speed falls with it.
- **Sound channel axis.** The depth of minimum sound speed, often near 1000 m at mid latitudes and much shallower toward the poles.
- **Deep isothermal layer.** Temperature is nearly constant and pressure raises sound speed steadily to the seabed.

Two more terms matter for long ranges. The **critical depth** is the deep depth where sound speed climbs back to its maximum value near the surface. Water below it is **depth excess**. Unit by unit, you will see why having some matters.

## Where profiles come from

A prediction needs a profile at the sensor and along every bearing it looks. There are three sources, and they differ in how current they are.

- **Climatology.** Gridded averages of decades of observations for each month and place. Always available. Blind to today’s weather, eddies and fronts.
- **Ocean forecast models.** Numerical models that assimilate satellite and float data. They carry fronts and eddies in roughly the right place, with errors that grow in complex water.
- **In situ measurement.** A probe dropped at the time and place, such as an expendable bathythermograph, which measures temperature against depth. It is exact for one point at one moment.

The ocean moves under all of them. Surface heating on a calm afternoon can erase a mixed layer in hours. Internal waves heave the thermocline up and down by tens of meters. A front can change the whole profile within a few kilometers.

:::pipeline
A profile arrives as pairs of depth and speed, or depth, temperature and salinity to be converted. Check that it reaches the seabed, since engines extrapolate what is missing. Record its source and time with every product computed from it.
:::

# How sound bends

## Rays bend toward slower water

Picture a wavefront moving through water whose sound speed changes with depth. The part of the front in faster water gets ahead, the front tilts, and the path curves **toward the slower water**. That single rule predicts every path in this unit.

- Sound speed **increasing with depth**: rays bend **up**.
- Sound speed **decreasing with depth**: rays bend **down**.

The bending is gentle. Where sound speed changes at a steady rate $g$, a ray follows the arc of a circle of radius

$$
R = \frac{c}{g \cos\theta_0}
$$

In the deep isothermal layer $g$ is about 0.016 per second, which gives a radius near 90 km. Rays are almost straight over short distances and decisively curved over long ones.

:::key
Sound is lazy in a particular way: it turns toward the slow water. A minimum in the profile is therefore a place sound keeps returning to. A maximum is a place sound avoids.
:::

## Surface duct and shadow zone

In the mixed layer sound speed rises slightly with depth, so rays bend up. They reach the surface, reflect and bend up again. Energy launched at shallow angles is trapped in a **surface duct**, hopping along the surface for long distances.

Rays launched more steeply cross the bottom of the layer into the thermocline, where the gradient reverses and bends them sharply down. Between the trapped rays above and the diving rays below lies a region that no direct ray enters: the **shadow zone**.

::play{preset="surface-duct"}

The shadow zone is the classic hiding place. A target a short distance below the layer can be nearly undetectable to a sonar in the layer, and the reverse is equally true.

Two limits on the duct:

- It only traps shallow angles, typically within a degree or two of horizontal. The **limiting ray** is the steepest one that still turns before leaving the layer.
- It only traps short wavelengths. A duct of depth $H$ meters stops working below roughly $f \approx 1.8 \times 10^{5} / H^{1.5}$ Hz. A 100 m layer traps 3.5 kHz well and 100 Hz hardly at all.

:::try
Change the mixed layer depth from 100 m to 30 m and watch the duct weaken. Then drag the source below the layer. The energy that was skipping along the surface now dives.
:::

## Deep paths

In deep water, rays that leave the upper ocean heading down are bent back up by the pressure gradient, provided the water is deep enough for them to turn before they hit the seabed. They return to the surface together, far from the source, in a **convergence zone**.

- The first zone is typically 55 to 65 km out at mid latitudes, and the pattern repeats at multiples of that range.
- Each zone is a ring only a few kilometers wide, with a gain of several decibels over simple spreading.
- Zones need **depth excess**. If the seabed is shallower than the critical depth, the rays strike bottom first and no zone forms. The water is then **bottom limited**.

Other deep paths are worth knowing by name:

- **Bottom bounce.** Steep rays reflect from the seabed and return to the surface at intermediate ranges, filling some of the gap before the first zone. How well depends on bottom loss.
- **Deep sound channel.** Rays that stay near the axis and never touch a boundary. Very low loss, and only useful to sources and receivers near that depth.
- **Reliable acoustic path.** From a very deep sensor, steep paths reach the surface with no boundary interaction, giving steady coverage out to a few tens of kilometers regardless of the surface layers.

:::operator
A convergence zone is a narrow annulus, with a wide ring of little or no coverage between it and the direct path. On a plan view it appears as a thin ring far from the sensor. It is real, and so is the gap.
:::

# Boundaries and shallow water

## The sea surface

The surface is almost a perfect reflector, because air is so much lighter than water that nearly all the sound is turned back. Two things complicate that simple picture.

First, the reflection **inverts the pressure**. Near the surface, the direct path and the surface-reflected path arrive almost together with opposite signs and cancel. A source or receiver very close to the surface is therefore inefficient at low frequency. The interference pattern this creates is called the Lloyd mirror effect.

Second, the surface is rough. Waves scatter some of the reflected energy out of the forward direction, which counts as **loss** to the path and as **reverberation** to the receiver. Breaking waves inject bubbles, which absorb and scatter further. Both effects grow with wind speed and with frequency.

:::pipeline
Wind speed feeds three terms at once: surface loss inside transmission loss, surface scattering strength inside reverberation, and wind noise inside ambient noise. One stale wind value corrupts all three in the same direction.
:::

## The seabed

The seabed is not a mirror. Some sound reflects, some passes into the sediment and is lost, and some scatters. What happens depends on the sediment and on the **grazing angle**, the angle between the ray and the bottom.

For a sediment in which sound travels faster than in water, such as sand, there is a **critical angle**. Below it nearly all the energy reflects. Above it a large share enters the bottom.

| Bottom | Critical angle | Low-angle loss | Steep-angle loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rock | large | very low | moderate |
| Sand | about 25 to 30° | low | high |
| Silt | about 10 to 15° | moderate | high |
| Mud | small or none | moderate | very high |

The pattern to remember: hard bottoms reflect well and soft bottoms absorb. Shallow grazing angles survive and steep ones do not.

:::pitfall
Bottom type is the least certain input to most predictions. Databases assign a province to a wide area from sparse samples. When a prediction over a soft bottom disagrees with what the sonar sees, suspect the bottom before the engine.
:::

## Shallow water, summer and winter

On a continental shelf, a hundred or two hundred meters deep, every path meets a boundary. The season decides which boundary and how often.

**Summer.** The sun builds a warm surface layer over cooler water. Sound speed falls with depth, every ray bends **down**, and sound is driven into the seabed again and again. Range is short and is set almost entirely by bottom loss.

::play{preset="shallow-summer"}

**Winter.** Storms mix the column top to bottom. Temperature is uniform, pressure makes sound speed rise slightly with depth, and rays bend gently **up**. Energy travels along the surface and touches the bottom less. Ranges can be several times longer than in summer, limited now by surface roughness.

:::try
Switch the playground between the summer and winter shelf. Count the bottom-bounce rays in each. Then in summer, drag the source from above the thermocline to below it and see which depths it reaches.
:::

## Range dependence

So far the ocean has been the same at every range. It is not, and two kinds of change matter most.

**Bathymetry.** A ray reflecting from a sloping bottom changes its angle by twice the slope. Going **upslope**, each bounce makes the ray steeper, until it exceeds the critical angle and its energy is absorbed. Going **downslope**, each bounce flattens the ray, which can feed energy into a deep channel.

::play{preset="upslope"}

**Water masses.** Crossing a front or an eddy changes the profile along the path. A duct on one side may not exist on the other.

Engines handle this by computing along **radials**. For each bearing from the sensor, the environment is sampled along that line and a two-dimensional problem in range and depth is solved. This is called N by 2D. It ignores sound that bends sideways, which is acceptable in most water and wrong around steep slopes and canyons.

:::pipeline
The number and spacing of radials is a real modeling choice. Too few, and a seamount between two radials does not exist. The plan view then interpolates across it and shows smooth coverage where there is a shadow.
:::
