# Vertical angles

## D/E: depression and elevation

The vertical direction of a ray or a beam is given by its **D/E angle**: its angle above or below the horizontal. D/E stands for depression and elevation. You will also hear it said as declination and elevation.

- **Elevation** points up, toward the surface.
- **Depression** points down, toward the seabed.
- Zero is horizontal.

The trouble is the sign. Two conventions are in use, and both are reasonable.

| Convention | Up | Down | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Up positive | + | − | Many system and display conventions |
| Down positive | − | + | Ray engines where depth increases downward, BELLHOP among them |

Neither is wrong. What is wrong is passing a value from one to the other without converting, and nothing in the number itself warns you.

:::

:::pipeline
Treat the D/E sign convention as part of the data, like a unit. Record it once in metadata for every product that carries angles, convert at the boundary where the engine’s output enters your system, and use one convention everywhere after that.
:::

## Grazing, incidence and launch angles

Several angles describe the same ray. Mixing them up is easy because each is natural in its own context.

- **Launch angle.** The D/E of a ray as it leaves the source.
- **Grazing angle.** The angle between a ray and a boundary it strikes, measured from the boundary surface. A ray skimming the bottom has a small grazing angle.
- **Angle of incidence.** The same thing measured from the perpendicular to the boundary. It is 90° minus the grazing angle. Optics and some bottom-loss tables use it.

Underwater acoustics almost always uses grazing angle, because the interesting rays are the shallow ones.

On a flat bottom the grazing angle equals the ray’s D/E where it hits. On a slope it does not:

$$
\theta_{grazing} = \theta_{ray} - \alpha_{slope}
$$

with both angles measured the same way. A ray descending at 10° onto a bottom that rises toward it at 3° strikes at a grazing angle of 13°.

:::pitfall
A bottom-loss or scattering table indexed by grazing angle, read with an angle of incidence, returns the value for the wrong end of the curve: low loss where it should be high. Check the axis label of every angle-indexed table.
:::

## Launch angle versus arrival angle

A path has a D/E at each end. The **launch angle** is its direction leaving the source. The **arrival angle** is its direction at the receiver. Through a changing sound speed profile, the two are generally different.

There is a second subtlety at the receiver. An angle can describe the direction the sound is **travelling**, or the direction the receiver must **look** to see it. They are opposites. Sound travelling downward as it arrives has come from above, so the receiver looks up.

| Sound is travelling | It came from | Receiver steers |
|---|---|---|
| Downward | Above | Up, elevation |
| Upward | Below | Down, depression |

Engines usually report direction of travel. Beam patterns are defined by look direction. The conversion is one sign change, and it is separate from the up-positive or down-positive choice. A value can therefore be wrong by one flip, or doubly wrong and accidentally right.

:::key
For every angle in an eigenray table, know three things: which end of the path it belongs to, whether it is direction of travel or direction of look, and which way is positive.
:::

# Snell’s law geometry

## Snell’s law in one line

Along any ray, in water where sound speed depends only on depth, one quantity never changes:

$$
\frac{\cos\theta}{c} = \text{constant}
$$

Here $\theta$ is the ray’s angle from the horizontal and $c$ is the sound speed where the ray is. This is Snell’s law in the form acoustics uses.

It lets you find a ray’s angle at any depth from its angle at one depth:

$$
\cos\theta_2 = \frac{c_2}{c_1}\cos\theta_1
$$

A ray leaving horizontally where the speed is 1500 m/s, at a depth where the speed is 1490 m/s, has $\cos\theta_2 = 1490/1500$, so $\theta_2 = 6.6°$. Moving into slower water steepens the ray. Moving into faster water flattens it.

:::math
The constant has a physical meaning. Its reciprocal, $c_v = c/\cos\theta$, is the **vertex speed**: the sound speed at the depth where this ray would be horizontal. Every ray carries its vertex speed with it.
:::

## Turning depths and limiting rays

A ray moving into faster water flattens until it is horizontal, then turns back. That happens exactly where the local sound speed equals the ray’s vertex speed:

$$
c(z_{turn}) = \frac{c_0}{\cos\theta_0}
$$

If no depth has a speed that high, the ray does not turn. It hits a boundary instead.

This gives the **limiting ray** of a duct at once. In a surface duct, sound speed is $c_s$ at the surface and reaches its maximum $c_L$ at the layer depth. A ray leaving the surface stays trapped only if it turns before the layer depth, so the steepest trapped ray has

$$
\cos\theta_{lim} = \frac{c_s}{c_L}
$$

For small angles this is close to $\theta_{lim} \approx \sqrt{2\,\Delta c / c}$ in radians. With a difference of 1.6 m/s across the layer, $\theta_{lim} \approx \sqrt{2 \times 1.6 / 1500} = 0.046$ rad, about 2.6°.

:::key
Ducts trap only a narrow fan of angles, a degree or two either side of horizontal. A beam steered or shaped to put its energy inside that fan uses the duct. Energy outside it leaves.
:::

## Ray curvature and skip distance

Where sound speed changes linearly with depth at a rate $g$ in units of per second, a ray is an arc of a circle of radius

$$
R = \frac{c_v}{g}
$$

where $c_v$ is the ray’s vertex speed. For $g = 0.016\ \text{s}^{-1}$ and $c \approx 1500$ m/s the radius is about 94 km.

A useful result follows for the surface duct. The limiting ray leaves the surface, just grazes the layer depth $H$, and returns to the surface after a horizontal distance called the **skip distance**:

$$
X = \sqrt{8RH}
$$

A 100 m layer gives $X = \sqrt{8 \times 94{,}000 \times 100} \approx 8.7$ km. The duct is touched by its steepest trapped ray about every 9 km, and more often by shallower ones.

:::operator
Skip distance tells you how quickly surface conditions act on ducted sound. A ray that meets the surface every few kilometers loses a little to roughness each time. Over tens of kilometers, a rough sea drains a duct.
:::

# Range, time and horizontal angles

## Slant range, horizontal range and time

Three distances describe a source and a point in the water.

- **Horizontal range** $r$: the distance along the surface.
- **Depth difference** $\Delta z$.
- **Slant range** $R$: the straight-line distance, $R = \sqrt{r^2 + \Delta z^2}$.

At long range in the open ocean the difference between slant and horizontal range is negligible. Directly beneath a sensor it is everything.

Sound takes $t = R / c$ seconds to travel a path of length $R$. For an echo, the round trip takes $t = 2R/c$, so at 1500 m/s:

- one second of echo time is **750 m** of range
- one kilometer of range is **1.33 s** of echo time

Range units vary by community. Keep the conversions at hand:

| | meters |
|---|---|
| 1 kiloyard (kyd) | 914.4 |
| 1 nautical mile (nmi) | 1852 |
| 1 km | 1000 |

A nautical mile is very nearly 2 kiloyards (2.025), which is why both survive in the same documents.

:::pitfall
Range computed from echo time assumes a path and a sound speed. A bottom-bounce echo has travelled farther than the horizontal range it came from, so a straight-line conversion places the target too far away.
:::

## Bearing: true and relative

Horizontal direction is a **bearing**, measured clockwise in degrees.

- **True bearing** is measured from true north. It is written with three digits and a T: 045 °T.
- **Relative bearing** is measured from the platform’s own bow. Dead ahead is 000 °R, the starboard beam is 090 °R.

They are related by the platform’s heading:

$$
\text{true bearing} = \text{heading} + \text{relative bearing} \pmod{360}
$$

An array’s beams are fixed to the array, so they are born as relative bearings. A sensor reports “beam 14”. That becomes a relative bearing through the array’s geometry, and a true bearing only once the heading at that moment is known.

:::pipeline
Anything measured per beam, including measured beam noise and measured reverberation, is in the sensor’s frame at the time of measurement. To draw it on a map you need the heading from that same time, not the current one. A platform that has turned since the measurement carries its noise picture with it in relative bearing and leaves it behind in true bearing.
:::

## Aspect angle

**Aspect** describes how the target is turned relative to the line of sight. It is the angle at the target between its bow and the direction to the observer.

- 0°: the observer sees the **bow**.
- 90° and 270°: the observer sees a **beam**, the long side.
- 180°: the observer sees the **stern**.

It follows from the bearing and the target’s heading:

$$
\text{aspect} = (\text{bearing from target to observer}) - (\text{target heading}) \pmod{360}
$$

and the bearing from target to observer is the bearing from observer to target plus 180°.

Aspect matters because target strength changes by more than 10 dB between beam and bow. It is also the one input a prediction almost never knows, because it depends on which way the target has chosen to point.

In a bistatic geometry there are two aspects: one for the source and one for the receiver.

:::
