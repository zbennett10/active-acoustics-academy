# Ambient noise

## Sources of ambient noise

**Ambient noise** is the sound already in the sea before the sonar does anything. It comes from many sources, and each owns a part of the spectrum.

| Source | Where it dominates | What controls it |
|---|---|---|
| Distant shipping | 10 Hz to a few hundred Hz | Traffic density, propagation to the shipping lanes |
| Wind and waves | A few hundred Hz to 50 kHz | Wind speed, through breaking waves and bubbles |
| Rain | 1 to 20 kHz when present | Rain rate |
| Biology | Narrow bands, by place and season | Snapping shrimp, whales, fish choruses |
| Ice | Polar regions | Cracking and ridging |
| Thermal agitation | Above about 50 kHz | Temperature of the water itself |

The classic summary is the set of **Wenz curves**, which plot these contributions against frequency. Active sonars working between 1 and 10 kHz live mostly in the wind-noise region, with shipping underneath at the low end and rain as an occasional intruder.

:::key
At active sonar frequencies, ambient noise is largely a weather report. Wind speed is the single most useful number.
:::

## Reading the spectrum

Noise is described by its **spectrum level**: the level in a one-hertz band, in dB re 1 µPa²/Hz, plotted against frequency.

::play

A few rules of thumb hold well enough to check a number against:

- In the wind-driven region the spectrum falls about **5 to 6 dB per octave**, roughly 17 dB per decade, as frequency rises.
- Each doubling of wind speed raises it about **5 to 7 dB**.
- At 1 kHz, expect roughly 55 dB in light winds and 70 dB in a gale.
- Heavy rain can add 10 to 20 dB between 1 and 10 kHz and flatten the spectrum.
- Shipping noise varies by 20 dB or more between a quiet basin and a busy lane.

:::try
Raise the wind from 5 to 30 knots and watch the total curve lift above 300 Hz while the left side stays put. Then add rain. Move the processor band from 1 kHz to 8 kHz and read how the band level changes.
:::

:::pipeline
A modeled ambient noise value is usually a spectrum level at one frequency. The processor needs a band level. The conversion, $+10 \log B$, belongs to the processor, which is one reason noise is a per-processor quantity.
:::

## Directionality

Models often treat ambient noise as **isotropic**, the same from every direction. It is a convenient fiction.

**In the vertical.** Wind noise is made at the surface, so near the surface it arrives mostly from above. Distant shipping noise arrives by long, shallow paths, close to horizontal. In deep water there can even be a quiet notch at exactly horizontal, where no long-range path from the surface arrives.

**In the horizontal.** Shipping noise comes from where the ships are: a lane, a port approach, a single nearby vessel. A storm on one side is louder than calm water on the other.

The array turns this directionality into something that matters. A beam looking at a noisy sector hears more than one looking at a quiet sector. The difference can be 10 or 20 dB, on the same sensor at the same moment.

:::operator
On a noise-limited day, detection range is not one number. It is a range for each bearing, shorter toward the traffic and longer away from it. A plan view that is a perfect circle was probably built from isotropic noise.
:::

# Self noise

## What self noise is

**Self noise** is noise the sonar’s own platform makes. It arrives at the hydrophones without ever having been ambient.

- **Machinery noise.** Engines, pumps and gears, reaching the array through the hull and the water. Dominant at low speed. Mostly tonal and low in frequency.
- **Flow noise.** Turbulent water moving over the array or its dome. Grows very fast with speed.
- **Propeller noise.** Cavitation from the platform’s own screws, heard through the water and reflected from the surface and bottom.
- **Electrical noise.** The floor of the hydrophones and amplifiers. Normally well below everything else, and the limit only in very quiet conditions.

Self noise is a property of the **platform and its speed**, not the ocean. Two different ships in the same water have the same ambient noise and different self noise.

:::key
Ambient noise is the sea’s. Self noise is yours. A prediction needs both, and they respond to different controls: one to weather and traffic, the other to speed and platform.
:::

## Self noise against speed

At low speed self noise is roughly constant, set by machinery. Above a certain speed flow noise takes over and climbs steeply, on the order of 1.5 to 2 dB for each knot.

The consequence is a **breakpoint speed**. Below it the sonar is limited by ambient noise and its own speed costs nothing. Above it every extra knot shortens detection range.

This creates the oldest trade in sonar operations:

- Go fast and cover ground, with a deafened sonar.
- Go slow and hear well, covering little.

Designs attack the problem directly. Towed arrays sit far behind the ship, away from its machinery and propeller. Variable depth sonars lower the array out of the surface layer and the wake. Sonobuoys and fixed sensors have almost no self noise at all.

:::operator
Sprint and drift is this trade made into a tactic: move quickly with the sonar degraded, then slow down and listen properly.
:::

## Noise limited or reverberation limited

The masking term in the active sonar equation is noise and reverberation combined. Which one dominates decides what will help.

**Noise limited.** The echo competes with noise. More source level, more array gain, a quieter platform and a longer pulse all help. This is the state at long range and late in the ping cycle, after the reverberation has decayed.

**Reverberation limited.** The echo competes with the sonar’s own backscatter. Source level does nothing. What helps is a smaller resolution cell, a narrower beam, or Doppler separation. This is the state at short and medium range, in shallow water, and at high source level.

The next unit treats reverberation in full. For now, note the boundary between them: the range at which the falling reverberation curve meets the flat noise floor.

:::pitfall
Raising source level to reach further works until the sonar becomes reverberation limited at that range. Past that point the extra power buys nothing except being heard at greater distance by others.
:::

# Noise at the beam

## From spectrum level to beam noise

The sonar equation needs noise at the same point where the echo is measured: at the output of a beam, in the processor’s band. Three steps get there from an ambient spectrum level.

1. **Spectrum level.** From a model or database at the processor’s center frequency: $NSL$, in dB re 1 µPa²/Hz.
2. **Band level.** Collect the whole processor band of width $B$:

$$
NL = NSL + 10 \log_{10} B
$$

3. **Beam level.** Apply the array’s rejection of noise from other directions. For isotropic noise that is the directivity index:

$$
N_{beam} = NSL + 10 \log_{10} B - DI
$$

Then add self noise, as a power sum.

A worked case: spectrum level 58 dB, band 400 Hz, directivity index 20 dB.

$$
N_{beam} = 58 + 26 - 20 = 64\ \text{dB re 1 µPa}
$$

:::pipeline
All three inputs depend on the processor: the spectrum level through its center frequency, the bandwidth directly, and the directivity index through the wavelength. A sensor with four processors has four modeled beam noise values, even in perfectly isotropic noise.
:::

## Measured beam noise

A sonar does not have to model its noise. It can listen to it.

**Measured beam noise** is the level observed at the output of each beam, in each processor’s band, when no echo or reverberation is present: before a ping, or late in the cycle after reverberation has died away.

It contains everything a model has to guess:

- the real ambient noise, with its real directionality
- every ship in the area, on the beams that point at them and in the sidelobes of the rest
- the platform’s self noise at its present speed
- the true array gain, including failed hydrophones and adaptive processing

It is organized the way the sonar is: **per sensor, per processor, per beam**, with a time stamp.

::play

:::try
The dashed circle is the isotropic model. The wedges are what the beams measure. Drag a ship around the compass and see which beams it raises. Then lower the sidelobe level from −25 to −40 dB and watch the leakage into the other beams disappear.
:::

## Using measured beam noise correctly

Measured beam noise is better than modeled noise for the moment it was taken. It is also easy to misuse.

- **Do not subtract directivity index.** The measurement is already at the beam output. It **replaces** the whole expression $NSL + 10\log B - DI$. Subtracting DI from it counts the array’s gain twice and makes the sonar look 20 dB better than it is.
- **Match the band.** A measurement made in one bandwidth and used for a processor with another must be adjusted by $10 \log (B_{new}/B_{measured})$, and only if the spectrum is reasonably flat between them.
- **Beam is not bearing.** Convert beams to true bearing with the heading at the time of measurement.
- **Make sure it is noise.** A measurement taken too soon after a ping contains reverberation.
- **It ages.** Ships move, the platform changes speed, the wind shifts. A value from an hour ago may describe a different sea.
- **It is local.** It says nothing about noise at another sensor’s position or depth.

:::pitfall
The double-counting error is hard to see because it makes predictions look good. If switching from modeled to measured noise *improves* signal excess by roughly the directivity index on every beam, the measurement has had DI subtracted from it.
:::

:::operator
The best use is both together. Measured beam noise sets the level and the directional picture now. The model explains it, extends it to other depths and sensors, and takes over when the measurement is stale.
:::
