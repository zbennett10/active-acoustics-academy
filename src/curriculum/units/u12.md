# Waveforms

## CW pulses

The simplest pulse is a burst of a single tone: **continuous wave**, CW, switched on for a time $T$.

A CW pulse is good at one thing and poor at another.

**Doppler.** A tone of duration $T$ has a spectrum about $1/T$ hertz wide. An echo from a moving target comes back shifted in frequency, and a long tone can measure that shift finely. A one-second pulse resolves about 1 Hz. At 3.5 kHz that is a difference in closing speed of 0.2 m/s, less than half a knot.

**Range.** The pulse occupies $cT/2$ of range. A one-second pulse cannot separate two things closer than 750 m, and its reverberation cell is 750 m deep.

| CW pulse length | Range cell | Speed resolution at 3.5 kHz |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 s | 75 m | 4.2 kn |
| 0.5 s | 375 m | 0.8 kn |
| 2 s | 1500 m | 0.2 kn |

The two pull against each other. Lengthening the pulse sharpens Doppler and blurs range.

:::key
CW is the waveform for targets that are **moving**. It finds them by their Doppler shift, even inside heavy reverberation. Against a target with no motion along the line of sight it has nothing to offer but a very large reverberation cell.
:::

## FM pulses

A **frequency modulated**, FM, pulse sweeps across a band of width $B$ during its duration $T$. The receiver correlates what arrives against a copy of the sweep. An echo produces a sharp peak at the instant it lines up with the copy.

The width of that peak is set by the bandwidth, not the duration:

$$
\Delta r = \frac{c}{2B}
$$

A 400 Hz sweep resolves 1.9 m in range, whether it lasts a tenth of a second or two seconds. The pulse can therefore be long, to carry plenty of energy, and still resolve finely. This is **pulse compression**, and the ratio of the transmitted length to the compressed length is the time-bandwidth product $BT$.

Two sweep shapes are common.

- **LFM**, linear: frequency changes at a steady rate. Simple and widely used.
- **HFM**, hyperbolic: the period, not the frequency, changes at a steady rate. A Doppler-shifted HFM echo is still an HFM sweep, displaced in time, so the correlator still responds fully. HFM is called **Doppler tolerant**.

:::key
FM is the waveform for **range** and for **reverberation**. Its small range cell holds little reverberation. It pays by knowing almost nothing about target speed.
:::

## The ambiguity surface

One picture summarizes what a waveform can and cannot do. Imagine an echo that arrives at a slightly wrong time and with a slightly wrong Doppler shift, and ask how strongly the processor still responds. Plot that response against both errors. The result is the **ambiguity surface**.

::play

- A **CW** pulse gives a ridge that is narrow in speed and wide in range.
- An **LFM** pulse gives a narrow ridge that is tilted: an error in speed looks like an error in range. This is range-Doppler coupling.
- An **HFM** pulse gives a similar tilted ridge that keeps its height at high speeds, where the LFM ridge fades.

The volume under the surface is fixed for a given pulse energy. A waveform can move its ambiguity around. It cannot make it go away.

:::try
Choose CW and set the target speed to zero: the target sits inside the magenta reverberation band. Increase the speed until it clears the band. Then switch to HFM with 400 Hz, turn on the zoom, and compare how thin the ridge is in range.
:::

## Doppler and the reverberation ridge

An echo from a target closing at speed $v$ returns shifted in frequency by

$$
\Delta f = \frac{2 v f}{c}
$$

At 3.5 kHz that is **2.4 Hz per knot**. A handy general figure is 0.69 Hz per knot per kilohertz.

Reverberation comes from scatterers that are almost stationary, so it piles up near zero Doppler. It is not perfectly sharp:

- The sonar’s own motion shifts reverberation from each direction differently. Across a beam of finite width, that spreads it.
- Surface waves and drifting scatterers move a little.

The result is a **reverberation ridge** a few knots wide, centered on zero after the sonar’s own motion is removed. A target moving faster than the ridge’s half-width along the line of sight stands **clear** of it, in a region where only noise competes. A target inside the ridge competes with all the reverberation in its cell.

:::operator
A target can hide from a CW processor by turning so that its motion is across the line of sight: zero closing speed, beam aspect. That same turn presents its highest target strength to an FM processor. The two waveforms cover each other’s blind side.
:::

# The processing chain

## What a processor is

A **processor** is the complete path from hydrophone data to a displayed decision, for one waveform in one band. Its stages are:

1. **Beamforming.** Hydrophone signals to beams, at this band’s wavelength.
2. **Band filtering.** Keep the band of this waveform, discard the rest.
3. **Matched filtering.** Correlate against the transmitted waveform. For CW this is a bank of narrow Doppler filters. For FM it is a replica correlator.
4. **Detection.** Form the magnitude of the output.
5. **Normalization.** Divide by a local estimate of the background.
6. **Thresholding and display.**

A processor is defined by a short list of parameters: center frequency, bandwidth, pulse type and duration, the beam set, and its detection threshold or recognition differential.

:::pipeline
That list is the processor’s identity in a prediction system. Two entries that differ in any item are different processors with different predictions, even on the same array from the same ping.
:::

## Matched filtering and processing gain

A matched filter gives the highest possible signal-to-noise ratio for a known waveform in random noise. Its output ratio depends only on the echo’s **energy** $E$ and the noise spectrum level $N_0$:

$$
SNR_{out} = \frac{2E}{N_0}
$$

The shape of the waveform does not enter. Against noise, a one-second CW pulse and a one-second FM sweep of the same power are equal. What matters is how much energy was sent, which is power times duration.

Compared with the ratio in the full band at the input, the improvement is the **processing gain**:

$$
PG = 10 \log_{10}(BT)
$$

A one-second, 400 Hz sweep has 26 dB.

Against **reverberation** the story changes. Reverberation is made of the same waveform, so the matched filter cannot reject it by shape. What the filter does is shrink the range cell to $c/2B$, so less reverberation falls in each one. The gain against reverberation is again about $10 \log BT$ for FM, and none at all for CW unless Doppler separates the target.

:::key
Against noise, send energy: the waveform does not matter. Against reverberation, send bandwidth or use Doppler: the energy does not matter.
:::

## Normalization and constant false alarm rate

The background a sonar hears is far from constant. Reverberation falls by tens of decibels across one listening period, and noise differs from beam to beam. A fixed threshold would give a flood of false alarms early and none late.

A **normalizer** fixes this. Around each cell it estimates the local background from neighboring cells, leaving out the cell itself and a small guard band, and divides the cell by that estimate. The output is a ratio: this cell compared with its surroundings. A fixed threshold on that ratio gives a roughly **constant false alarm rate** everywhere.

The display therefore shows local signal-to-background, not level.

- An echo appears as a bright point however strong the reverberation behind it, provided it stands above it.
- The absolute level of reverberation is no longer visible on the normalized display.

:::pipeline
Measured reverberation and measured beam noise must be tapped **before** the normalizer. After it, the background has been divided out and every beam reads the same. Asking for the right tap point is the first question when a sonar is to supply measured data.
:::

## Continuous active sonar

Everything so far assumed a **pulsed** sonar: a short transmission, then a long silence to listen. A **continuous active sonar**, or high duty cycle sonar, transmits almost all the time, typically a long repeating sweep, and listens while it transmits.

Its attraction is update rate. A pulsed sonar with a 20 s ping interval reports a target’s position every 20 s. A continuous sonar can process sub-bands of its sweep separately and report every second or two, which helps a tracker hold a maneuvering target.

Published sea trials comparing the two in shallow water found detection performance in reverberation roughly comparable for the same band, with the continuous waveform’s advantage showing in tracking. The same work flags the costs:

- More reports also means more clutter reports, and more false tracks.
- The receiver must hear weak echoes during the direct transmission, a severe dynamic range problem that favors bistatic separation of source and receiver.
- Reverberation for continuous transmission is harder to model than for a pulse.

:::operator
Continuous active is a different trade, not a better sonar: more looks per minute at lower energy per look, in exchange for a harder false alarm problem.
:::

# One sensor, many processors

## Why a sensor carries several processors

No waveform is best at everything. So a sensor runs several processors at once, on the same hydrophones and often on the same ping.

- A **CW** processor to catch moving targets by Doppler.
- An **FM** processor to catch slow or beam-aspect targets by range resolution and to localize precisely.
- **Several pulse lengths**, long for distant search and short for close-in work, since a long pulse blinds the sonar at short range.
- **Several bands**, because propagation, noise and target strength all change with frequency.

A single transmission is often a train: a CW pulse followed by an FM sweep, each handled by its own processor on reception.

Each processor produces its own detections and deserves its own prediction. The target sits at one place. Whether it is seen depends on which processor is asked.

:::try

:::

## Per sensor or per processor

Sorting quantities into what belongs to the sensor and what belongs to the processor prevents a whole family of plumbing errors.

| Belongs to the **sensor** | Belongs to the **processor** |
|---|---|
| Position and depth | Center frequency and bandwidth |
| Heading and attitude | Waveform and pulse length |
| Array geometry | Beam pattern at this band, and so DI |
| Hydrophone health | Source level in this band |
| | Detection threshold or recognition differential |

Now look at the sonar equation with that table in hand.

- **Transmission loss** depends on frequency, through absorption, boundary loss and ducting. Per processor.
- **Target strength** depends on frequency and pulse resolution. Per processor.
- **Noise** depends on band and on directivity at that band. Per processor.
- **Reverberation** depends on effective pulse length and beamwidth. Per processor.
- **Detection threshold.** Per processor.

:::key
Almost nothing in the sonar equation is a property of the sensor alone. The sensor fixes the geometry. The processor fixes the numbers.
:::

:::pipeline
Processors that share a band can share one engine run, since loss depends on frequency and geometry and not on the waveform. That is the main computational saving available: group processors by band, compute loss once per band, then fan out.
:::

## Combining processors

With a prediction for each processor, a display has to decide what to show.

**Each processor separately.** The most honest and the most demanding. An operator sees that the FM mode covers one region and the CW mode another.

**Best of.** At each cell, show the highest signal excess among the processors. Simple, and correct for the question “is there some mode that would see it here”. It should also record *which* processor won, because that is what the operator has to select.

**Combined probability.** Treat processors as separate looks and combine: $1 - \prod(1 - P_i)$. This is higher than the best single processor. It overstates the truth to the extent that the processors share the same fluctuations, which they largely do: same path, same target, same instant.

:::pitfall
A best-of display built for one assumed target speed is misleading. The CW processors’ contribution depends entirely on Doppler. At zero closing speed the picture collapses to the FM coverage alone. Show the speed assumption with the display.
:::
