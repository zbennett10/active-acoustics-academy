# Fields

## Pairs

A multistatic system has several sources, several receivers or both. Its basic unit is still the bistatic pair. What changes is how many there are.

With $N$ sources and $M$ receivers, every source can be heard by every receiver:

$$
\text{pairs} = N \times M
$$

If the source platforms also receive, add $N$ monostatic pairs.

| Sources | Receivers | Bistatic pairs |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | 4 |
| 2 | 8 | 16 |
| 4 | 16 | 64 |

The count grows as a product, and so does everything that depends on it: coverage, opportunities to detect, false alarms, data, and the cost of predicting it all.

Receivers are cheap compared with sources. They are passive, small and silent. So practical fields have few sources and many receivers: a ship or a helicopter pinging while a line of sonobuoys listens, or a small number of source buoys among many receiving ones.

:::key
Adding one receiver to a field with $N$ sources adds $N$ new pairs. Adding one source to a field with $M$ receivers adds $M$. The expensive node buys the most.
:::

## Coverage

Each pair has its own region of positive signal excess, shaped like a Cassini oval with a blind strip along its baseline. A field’s coverage is all of those regions laid on top of one another.

::play{preset="barrier"}

The reason for a field is that the regions overlap usefully.

- One pair’s **blind zone** lies in the middle of another pair’s coverage.
- One pair’s **pinch**, where its oval narrows, is filled by its neighbor.
- A **hole** exists only where every pair fails at once.

Coverage is a design problem with real choices: how far apart to space the nodes, whether to make a line or a cluster, where to put the scarce sources.

:::try
Start from one pair and note the area covered. Switch to the ring and then the barrier. Now drag one receiver far from the others and see how little it adds alone, then drag it into a gap and see how much.
:::

## Aspect diversity

The strongest argument for a field is not area. It is that the target is viewed from many directions at once.

A monostatic sonar sees one aspect. If the target points its bow at it, target strength is 15 dB down and there is nothing to be done. In a field, each pair has its own pair of aspects. For the target to be weak to all of them it would have to be bow-on to every bisector, which is impossible when the nodes surround it.

Some pair will usually be near **specular**: its bisector close to the target’s beam. That pair sees the highest target strength the target has, regardless of which way the target is pointing.

:::try
Set target strength to By aspect. With one pair, sweep the heading through 360° and watch coverage swing between almost nothing and a wide band. Then load the barrier layout and sweep again. The coverage changes shape and never collapses.
:::

:::operator
A field converts the target’s aspect from a single gamble into an average. That is worth more than the same number of sensors working alone.
:::

# Fusion

## Combining probabilities

To turn many pairs into one statement about detection, combine their probabilities. If pair $i$ detects with probability $P_i$ and the pairs behave independently:

$$
P_{field} = 1 - \prod_i (1 - P_i)
$$

Three pairs at 40 percent each give $1 - 0.6^3 = 78$ percent.

Independence is more defensible here than for repeated pings of one sonar. Different pairs use different paths, see different aspects and have different noise. It is still not complete:

- Pairs that share a **source** share its outbound path and any error in it.
- Pairs that share a **receiver** share its noise.
- All pairs share the same ocean, and the same bad assumption about it.

Two simpler summaries are also used. **Best pair** shows the highest signal excess among pairs, and so answers whether any single pair would see the target well. **Pairs detecting** counts how many pairs have positive signal excess, a measure of how robust the coverage is.

:::pitfall
A field map that shows only combined probability can look healthy where it is fragile. Ninety percent built from six weak pairs is different from ninety percent built on one strong pair. The count of contributing pairs tells them apart.
:::

## Contacts, tracks and fusion

A prediction says where detections are possible. The system then has to turn real detections from many receivers into one picture. That is **fusion**, and it is the hard part of multistatics.

Each receiver reports **contacts**: an echo time and a bearing for a particular source’s ping. Each contact is an ellipse and a bearing line, with uncertainty in both. Fusion must:

1. **Associate.** Decide which contacts, from different pairs and different pings, are the same object.
2. **Localize.** Combine their ellipses and bearings. Ellipses from different pairs cross at steep angles, so the combined position can be much tighter than any one pair gives.
3. **Track.** Follow the object over time and reject contacts that do not move plausibly.

The difficulty is false alarms. Every pair generates its own clutter contacts. With 64 pairs there are 64 streams of them, and the number of possible wrong associations grows faster still.

The published literature here is extensive. Multi-hypothesis tracking, which keeps several possible associations alive until later data decides between them, is the standard approach. An international Multistatic Tracking Working Group has compared trackers on shared data sets for many years.

:::pipeline
Fusion can happen at the contact level, sending every contact to a central tracker, or at the track level, where each node tracks first and sends tracks. The first performs better and needs far more bandwidth. That choice shapes the whole data architecture.
:::

## Interference and scheduling

Several sources in one area interfere with each other.

- Every receiver hears the **direct blast** of every source. Each blanks the receiver for its duration.
- Reverberation from one source’s ping raises the background for echoes from another’s.
- A receiver must know **which source** produced an echo, or it places the contact on the wrong ellipse.

The remedies are forms of separation.

| Separation | How | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| In time | Sources ping in turn | Slower update from each source |
| In frequency | Each source has its own band | Band is divided among them |
| In waveform | Distinguishable sweeps or codes | Residual cross-talk |

A ping schedule is part of the field’s design, as much as node positions are.

:::operator
More sources is not simply better. Past a point, each added source costs the others listening time and adds to their reverberation. The ping plan decides whether a field’s sources help or hinder one another.
:::

# Planning and prediction

## Predicting a field

A field prediction is many bistatic predictions. Done naively, the cost is one pair at a time, with two propagation runs each: $2NM$ runs.

Reciprocity removes most of that. The loss between a node and a target position does not depend on which other node it is paired with. So:

- Run the engine **once per node**, per band: $N + M$ runs.
- For any pair, look up the source node’s field and the receiver node’s field at each target position.

For 4 sources and 16 receivers that is 20 runs, not 128.

Per pair, what remains is cheap arithmetic: add the two losses, look up target strength for that pair’s aspects, subtract that receiver’s noise and reverberation, and apply that pair’s blind zone.

:::pipeline
This is the central architectural fact about multistatic prediction. Loss fields are **per node**. Echo, signal excess and probability are **per pair**. Cache at the node level and the field recomputes quickly when one node moves: only that node’s field is stale.
:::

## Field design

A few principles cover most layouts.

**Spacing follows the oval.** Find the equivalent range at which signal excess reaches zero for the expected conditions. Nodes much farther apart than that leave gaps. Nodes much closer waste coverage in overlap.

**The environment sets the scale.** In poor conditions the ovals shrink and the same number of buoys covers less. A layout planned for winter fails in summer.

**Barriers and areas differ.** A barrier must have no gap along its length and needs little depth. An area search needs every point covered at least once.

**Put sources where their blind zones are covered.** The strip along each baseline should fall inside another pair’s coverage.

**Use depth.** Nodes above and below the layer cover each other’s shadow zones. A field need not be at one depth.

:::operator
Plan for the target you least want to miss: the depth with the worst loss, the aspect with the lowest target strength. A field that covers that case covers the rest.
:::

## What an operator needs from the display

A multistatic prediction produces far more than can be shown. A useful display makes choices.

- **Combined coverage first.** The probability that the field detects, as one map.
- **Then why.** Which pairs contribute at a chosen point, and how many.
- **Holes made obvious.** Gaps, pinches and blind zones should stand out, not blend into a gradient.
- **Assumptions on the face of it.** Target depth, target aspect or heading, target speed for Doppler processors, and which noise and reverberation were measured.
- **What moves it.** How coverage changes if one node is lost, or if the target goes deep.

:::key
The display’s job in a multistatic system is to turn hundreds of pair-level results into the few things a planner can act on: where the gaps are, why, and what would close them.
:::
