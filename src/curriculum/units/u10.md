# Where reverberation comes from

## Three kinds of reverberation

A ping does not meet only the target. It meets everything in the sea that can scatter sound, and each scatterer sends a little back. The sum of all those small returns is **reverberation**.

It is sorted by where the scatterers are.

- **Surface reverberation.** From the rough sea surface and the bubble layer beneath it. Grows with wind speed.
- **Bottom reverberation.** From the roughness of the seabed and from inhomogeneities just inside it. Depends on bottom type and usually dominates in shallow water.
- **Volume reverberation.** From within the water: fish, plankton and their gas bubbles. Often concentrated in the **deep scattering layer**, which rises toward the surface at night and sinks by day.

Unlike noise, reverberation is made by the ping. It has the ping’s frequency, starts when the ping starts, and decays as the sound travels outward to scatterers that are farther and farther away.

:::key
Reverberation is echo from things you are not looking for. At any instant after the ping, what you hear came from all the scatterers at one particular range.
:::

## Scattering strength

The ocean’s tendency to scatter is described by a **scattering strength**: the target strength of a unit of scattering material.

- For a boundary, $S_s$ or $S_b$ in dB is the target strength of **one square meter** of surface or bottom.
- For the volume, $S_v$ in dB is the target strength of **one cubic meter** of water.

**Bottom.** A widely used form is Lambert’s law, in which scattering falls off with the sines of the incoming and outgoing grazing angles:

$$
S_b = 10 \log_{10}\mu + 10 \log_{10}(\sin\theta_{in}\,\sin\theta_{out})
$$

The constant $10 \log \mu$ describes the bottom. A classic value from Mackenzie’s measurements is −27 dB. Rough rock can be 10 dB higher and smooth mud 10 dB lower. For backscatter both angles are equal and the second term is $20 \log \sin\theta$.

**Surface.** The Chapman-Harris formula gives surface backscattering strength from wind speed, frequency and grazing angle. Typical values are −50 to −30 dB, rising with wind and with grazing angle.

**Volume.** Values run from below −90 dB in empty water to about −60 dB inside a dense scattering layer.

:::pitfall
Lambert’s law drops steeply at small grazing angles. At 5° the angle term alone is −21 dB, and at 1° it is −35 dB. Long-range bottom reverberation therefore depends heavily on exactly what grazing angles the propagation delivers, which is why reverberation needs eigenrays and not just loss.
:::

## The reverberation level equation

Reverberation obeys the same budget as an echo, with the target replaced by a patch of ocean.

For a boundary:

$$
RL = SL - TL_{out} - TL_{back} + S + 10 \log_{10} A
$$

$S$ is the scattering strength and $A$ is the **area** of boundary contributing at that instant. That area is a strip as wide as the beam and as deep in range as half the pulse:

$$
A = \frac{c\tau}{2}\;\Phi\; r
$$

with $\tau$ the pulse length, $\Phi$ the beamwidth in radians and $r$ the range.

For the volume, replace area with the **volume** of a shell, $V = \frac{c\tau}{2}\,\Psi\,r^2$, where $\Psi$ is the beam’s solid angle.

Read the equation for what the sonar controls:

- $SL$ appears, as it does for the echo.
- $\tau$ appears: a longer pulse insonifies more scatterers at once.
- $\Phi$ appears: a wider beam does the same.

:::math
For a matched-filtered FM pulse, the $\tau$ in this equation is not the transmitted duration. It is the **compressed** pulse length, $1/B$, where $B$ is the bandwidth. A one-second sweep of 400 Hz has the reverberation of a 2.5 ms pulse.
:::

# How reverberation behaves

## How reverberation decays

After a ping, reverberation falls with time as the sound reaches scatterers at greater range.

::play

Put simple spreading into the level equation and the trends appear.

- Two-way spherical loss contributes $-40 \log r$.
- A boundary strip grows as $r$, contributing $+10 \log r$. **Boundary reverberation falls as about $30 \log r$.**
- A volume shell grows as $r^2$, contributing $+20 \log r$. **Volume reverberation falls as about $20 \log r$.**
- An **echo** from a point target has no growing area. **It falls as $40 \log r$.**

That last line carries the lesson. The echo fades faster than the reverberation behind it. Against boundary reverberation the echo loses about 3 dB of margin each time range doubles, even though both are getting weaker.

:::try
Turn on the bottom, surface and volume parts. Raise the wind and watch only the surface curve move. Change the bottom type and watch only the bottom curve move. Then hover along the range axis and read the time for each range.
:::

## What changes reverberation and what does not

The ratio of echo to reverberation is what decides detection when reverberation is the limit. Subtract the reverberation equation from the echo equation: source level and loss cancel.

$$
EL - RL = TS - S - 10 \log_{10} A
$$

Everything that remains is a lever or a non-lever.

**Does not help:**

- **Source level.** It cancelled. Double the power and both rise 3 dB.

**Helps:**

- **Shorter effective pulse.** Halving $\tau$ gains 3 dB. Going from a 1 s CW pulse to a 400 Hz FM sweep gains $10 \log 400 = 26$ dB.
- **Narrower beam.** Halving the beamwidth gains 3 dB against boundary reverberation.
- **Vertical beam control.** Keeping energy off the boundary lowers $S$ through the grazing angle and removes steep paths altogether.
- **Doppler.** Reverberation comes from scatterers that are nearly still. A moving target’s echo is shifted in frequency and can be filtered away from it. Unit 12 covers this.

:::key
Against noise you want energy. Against reverberation you want resolution: in range, in angle or in Doppler.
:::

## From reverberation limited to noise limited

Reverberation decays and noise does not. Somewhere along the range axis the falling curve meets the flat one. That **crossover** divides the listening period in two.

- **Inside the crossover** the sonar is reverberation limited. Detection depends on target strength against scattering strength and cell size.
- **Beyond it** the sonar is noise limited. Detection depends on source level, loss and noise.

Where they are equal the masking is 3 dB above either one.

The crossover moves with every setting:

| Change | Crossover moves |
|---|---|
| More source level | Outward: reverberation rises, noise does not |
| Shorter effective pulse | Inward |
| Rougher bottom or more wind | Outward |
| More noise | Inward |

:::operator
Knowing which side of the crossover a target range lies on tells you what to change. Inside it, change the waveform or the beam. Outside it, change the power or the pulse length. Doing the opposite wastes the effort.
:::

## Clutter

Smooth reverberation is not the worst of it. The sea also contains discrete, strong scatterers: wrecks, rock outcrops, seamounts, pipelines, schools of fish. Each returns a compact echo that looks very much like a target. This is **clutter**.

Clutter differs from diffuse reverberation in ways that matter.

- It is **localized** in range and bearing, as a target is.
- It is often **persistent**, appearing ping after ping in the same place if it is fixed to the bottom.
- It makes the statistics of the background **heavy-tailed**: large values turn up far more often than a smooth noise-like model predicts.

The practical effect is false alarms. A detector set for a tolerable false alarm rate against smooth reverberation produces many more against clutter.

:::pipeline
A reverberation model predicts the smooth average. Clutter appears only if the model is given the features that cause it, which means high-resolution bathymetry and feature databases. Measured reverberation shows clutter directly, as bumps that repeat at fixed positions.
:::

# Modeled and measured reverberation

## Modeling reverberation

A reverberation model does for the whole ocean what the echo equation does for one target. For each instant after the ping it must:

1. Find every patch of surface, bottom and volume whose two-way travel time matches that instant.
2. Find the paths from source to patch and from patch to receiver, with their losses and angles.
3. Apply the transmit beam pattern at each launch angle and the receive pattern at each arrival angle.
4. Look up scattering strength at the grazing angles.
5. Multiply by the patch size and sum the power over all patches and path combinations.

The result is a curve of level against time for each receive beam and each processor.

Every input is uncertain, and the uncertain ones dominate:

- **Scattering strength** is known to within about 10 dB from a database, at best.
- **Bottom loss** shapes every bounce path to the patch.
- **Grazing angles** follow from the profile, and Lambert’s law magnifies small errors.

:::key
Modeled reverberation often has the right shape and the wrong level. The slope comes from propagation, which models do well. The level comes from scattering strength, which nobody knows well in advance.
:::

## Measured reverberation

After every ping, the sonar records exactly what a reverberation model tries to compute. **Measured reverberation** is the level at each beam output, in each processor, against time since the ping.

It has properties to keep clearly in mind.

- It is **reverberation plus noise**, and plus any echoes and clutter. Late in the record it is entirely noise.
- It is indexed by **time**. Converting to range needs a travel-time assumption, $r = ct/2$ for a monostatic sonar on a direct path.
- It is specific to the **ping that made it**: that source level, that waveform, that beam, that depth, that heading and that piece of ocean.
- It is at the **processor output**, after beamforming and matched filtering, so the processor’s effective pulse length is already in it.

## Model against measurement

Laying a measured curve over a modeled one is the most informative comparison in active sonar. How they differ says what is wrong.

| What you see | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Same shape, constant offset | Scattering strength, or a level calibration |
| Measured decays faster | More loss than modeled: bottom softer, surface rougher |
| Measured decays slower | Less loss, or a duct the model lacks |
| Agreement early, measured flat later | The measurement has reached the noise floor |
| Sharp bumps at fixed ranges | Clutter or bathymetric features |
| Offset on some beams only | Beam-dependent bottom, or a beam pattern error |

Fit only where the measurement is reverberation. Past the crossover it is noise, which carries no information about the seabed.

This comparison is also a research method. In reverberation inversion, the mismatch is used to estimate bottom properties. The results are useful and not unique: several combinations of bottom loss and scattering strength can explain the same curve.

:::pipeline
To compare the two honestly they must be at the same reference point. If the model gives reverberation at the hydrophone in a one-hertz band and the measurement is at the beam output after the matched filter, bring one to the other with band, directivity and processing gain before drawing any conclusion.
:::

## Using measured reverberation in a prediction

There are three ways to use a measurement, in rising order of ambition.

**Replace.** Use the measured curve directly as the masking term for the same sensor, processor, beam and settings. It is exact for the ping just sent. Since it already includes noise, it replaces the whole masking term, not reverberation alone.

**Calibrate.** Adjust the model’s scattering strength, and perhaps bottom loss, until the model reproduces the measurement. The corrected model can then predict for other target depths and slightly different conditions.

**Rescale.** Predict reverberation for a different setting from the measured one. The reverberation part scales in known ways:

$$
RL_{new} = RL_{old} + \Delta SL + 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\tau_{new}}{\tau_{old}}\right)
$$

Rescaling must be applied to reverberation alone. Remove the noise first, scale, then add the noise for the new processor back.

:::pitfall
Scaling a measured curve that includes noise lifts or lowers the noise floor along with the reverberation. A 10 dB increase in source level does not make the ocean 10 dB noisier.
:::

:::operator
Measured reverberation answers the question for the ping you sent. The model answers it for the ping you are thinking of sending. Use the first to earn trust in the second.
:::
