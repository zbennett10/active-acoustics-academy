# The equation

## The noise-limited form

Every unit so far has built one term. Now they meet.

Start with the echo. Sound leaves at the source level, pays loss on the way out, gains the target strength, and pays loss on the way back:

$$
EL = SL - TL_{out} - TL_{back} + TS
$$

When noise is what masks the echo, the signal-to-noise ratio at the beam output is the echo level minus the beam noise. With modeled isotropic noise:

$$
SNR = \underbrace{SL - 2TL + TS}_{\text{echo}} - \underbrace{(NL - DI)}_{\text{noise at the beam}}
$$

Detection needs that ratio to reach the detection threshold. The margin by which it does is the signal excess:

$$
SE = SL - 2TL + TS - (NL - DI) - DT
$$

This is the **noise-limited active sonar equation** for a monostatic sonar, where the loss out and the loss back are equal.

Every term is in decibels, and every term must refer to the same band and the same point in the receiver.

:::key
Read it as a sentence: what I sent, less what the ocean took twice, plus what the target gave back, compared with the noise my beam lets through and the margin my processor needs.
:::

## The reverberation-limited form

When reverberation is what masks the echo, noise drops out and reverberation takes its place:

$$
SE = SL - 2TL + TS - RL - DT
$$

Here $RL$ is the reverberation level at the beam output, in the processor’s band, at the time the echo arrives.

Now recall from unit 10 what $RL$ contains: $SL - 2TL' + S + 10 \log A$, where the loss is to the scattering patch. Substituting it:

$$
SE = TS - S - 10 \log_{10} A - (2TL - 2TL') - DT
$$

Source level has gone. If the target and the scattering patch are reached by similar paths, most of the loss has gone too. What is left is a comparison between the target and a patch of ocean:

- the target’s strength, $TS$
- against the ocean’s, $S + 10 \log A$

:::operator
In reverberation the question is not “is the echo loud enough” but “is the target a better scatterer than the piece of sea floor that shares its resolution cell”. Shrink the cell, and the sea floor’s side of that comparison shrinks with it.
:::

## One masking level

Real conditions are rarely purely one or the other. The general equation uses both, combined as powers:

$$
SE = SL - 2TL + TS - \left[\,(NL - DI) \oplus RL\,\right] - DT
$$

The bracket is the **masking level**:

$$
M = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(10^{(NL-DI)/10} + 10^{RL/10}\right)
$$

From unit 2 you know how it behaves. Where one term is 10 dB above the other it is the masking, to within half a decibel. Where they are equal, masking is 3 dB above either.

::play

:::try
Look at the top plot. The echo falls with range, reverberation falls more slowly, and noise is flat. Detection happens where the echo is above the dashed line, which is masking plus threshold. Turn reverberation off and see how much of the short-range picture it was controlling.
:::

:::pipeline
With measured data the bracket simplifies. Measured beam noise replaces $(NL - DI)$ whole. Measured reverberation, which already contains noise, replaces the entire bracket.
:::

# Signal excess

## Computing signal excess

Signal excess is one subtraction, done at every cell. A worked case makes the bookkeeping concrete.

A sonar pings at 220 dB. The target is where one-way loss is 78 dB, and its target strength is 12 dB. Beam noise in the processor band is 58 dB, reverberation at that time is 62 dB, and the detection threshold is 10 dB.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Echo level | 220 − 78 − 78 + 12 | 76 dB |
| Masking | 58 ⊕ 62 | 63.5 dB |
| Signal-to-masking ratio | 76 − 63.5 | 12.5 dB |
| Signal excess | 12.5 − 10 | **+2.5 dB** |

The target is detectable with somewhat better than even odds. With $\sigma = 8$ dB the probability of detection is $\Phi(2.5/8) = 62$ percent.

Change one thing. The target moves to where loss is 81 dB, 3 dB more each way. Echo level falls by 6 to 70 dB. Reverberation at the later time has dropped to 57 dB, so masking is $58 \oplus 57 = 60.5$ dB. Signal excess is $70 - 60.5 - 10 = -0.5$ dB.

:::key
Three decibels more one-way loss cost six in the echo. The sonar equation punishes loss twice, which is why propagation dominates active prediction.
:::

## Figure of merit

Rearranging the noise-limited equation with $SE = 0$ isolates the loss the sonar can afford:

$$
FOM = SL + TS - (NL - DI) - DT
$$

This **figure of merit** is the largest **two-way** loss at which detection is still 50 percent. Half of it is the allowable one-way loss.

Its value is that it separates the sonar and target from the ocean. Compute it once, then lay it on any loss curve: wherever $2TL$ is below the figure of merit, signal excess is positive.

With $SL = 220$, $TS = 12$, beam noise 58 and $DT = 10$, the figure of merit is 164 dB, so one-way loss up to 82 dB can be tolerated.

Two warnings.

- Some texts define the figure of merit without target strength, or as a one-way figure. Check before comparing numbers.
- It applies to the **noise-limited** case only. Reverberation depends on range, so no single number separates sonar from ocean when reverberation is the limit.

## From signal excess to detection range

Plot signal excess against range and the places where it is positive are where detection is likely. People often want to compress this to a single **detection range**. Sometimes that is fair. Often it is not.

A loss curve is not a steady climb. It can dip in a shadow zone and recover in a convergence zone. Signal excess then crosses zero several times, and the answer is a set of **detection zones**:

- a direct path zone from near the sensor out to some range
- a gap
- perhaps a bottom-bounce zone
- another gap
- a convergence zone annulus

“Range of the day” reports a single number from this, usually the outer edge of the first zone. It is a useful shorthand and a dangerous one. It hides the convergence zone beyond it, and it hides the fact that the first zone may itself have holes for other target depths.

:::operator
Ask for zones, not a range. Then ask how each zone changes with target depth, since the target will find the depth where the zone is smallest.
:::

# Bookkeeping

## Where each term is measured

The sonar equation is only valid if every term refers to the same place in the receiver chain and the same band. There are three natural reference points.

| Reference point | Echo | Noise |
|---|---|---|
| At a hydrophone | Echo level in the water | Spectrum level × bandwidth |
| At the beam output | Same echo, on the beam axis | Reduced by array gain |
| At the processor output | Raised by processing gain | In the processor’s resolution cell |

Any of them works. What does not work is an echo at one point compared with noise at another.

The common convention in prediction, and the one this curriculum uses, is the **beam output in the processor band**. Echo level is as it arrives. Noise is band level less directivity index. Reverberation is at the beam output. Whatever the processor does after that is carried by the detection threshold.

:::pitfall
Measured quantities come from wherever the sonar happens to tap them. Measured beam noise is at the beam output. Measured reverberation is usually after the matched filter. Before using either, move it to the convention the rest of the equation uses.
:::

## Processing gain: in the threshold or separate

Processing gain can be carried in two ways, and both are common.

**Inside the detection threshold.** DT is quoted as the ratio needed at the beam output in the processor band. A processor with 26 dB of gain that needs +10 dB at its output has a DT of −16 dB.

$$
SE = EL - M - DT, \qquad DT = -16
$$

**As a separate term.** DT is quoted at the processor output, and processing gain appears on its own.

$$
SE = EL - M + PG - DT, \qquad PG = 26,\ DT = +10
$$

Both give the same signal excess. Combining them, a table from one convention used in the equation of the other, is wrong by the full processing gain.

For reverberation there is a third place the same gain can hide: the effective pulse length inside $RL$. If reverberation was computed with the compressed pulse, the gain against reverberation is already taken and must not be applied again.

:::pipeline
Write the convention into the definition of each processor: what DT is relative to, and whether reverberation is supplied for the transmitted or the compressed pulse. A reader of the configuration should not need to ask.
:::

## Sensitivity: which term matters

Every decibel in the equation changes signal excess by one, or by two for one-way loss. They are not equally uncertain, and uncertainty is what matters.

| Term | Typical uncertainty | Effect on SE |
|---|---|---|
| Source level | 1 dB | 1 dB |
| Detection threshold | 2 to 3 dB | 2 to 3 dB |
| Noise, modeled | 5 dB or more | 5 dB |
| Noise, measured | 1 to 2 dB | 1 to 2 dB |
| Target strength | 5 to 10 dB | 5 to 10 dB |
| Transmission loss, one way | 3 to 5 dB | **6 to 10 dB** |
| Reverberation, modeled | 5 to 10 dB | 5 to 10 dB |
| Reverberation, measured | 2 dB | 2 dB |

The table says where effort is rewarded. Refining source level by half a decibel is pointless beside a 5 dB doubt about the bottom. Measurement turns two of the largest rows into two of the smallest.

How much a decibel is worth in range depends on the slope of the loss curve. Where loss climbs steeply, 5 dB moves detection range very little. Where it is nearly flat, as in a duct, 5 dB can halve it.

:::operator
A prediction is most fragile where the loss curve is flat and signal excess is near zero over a long stretch. That is where to state the answer with the most caution.
:::
