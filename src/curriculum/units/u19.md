# Uncertainty

## Where uncertainty comes from

A prediction combines many inputs, and each is known imperfectly. Sorting the doubts by size shows where attention belongs.

**The environment**

- **Sound speed profile.** Climatology misses today’s mixed layer depth, which can decide whether a duct exists at all. Errors here change the *shape* of the loss field, not just its level.
- **Bottom properties.** Loss and scattering strength over a wide area are inferred from sparse samples. Errors of 5 to 10 dB in bottom-interacting paths are ordinary.
- **Sea surface.** Wind forecasts are good. Their effect on loss and scattering is modeled only roughly.

**The target**

- **Aspect.** Unknown by nature. Worth 10 to 15 dB.
- **Depth.** Unknown, and chosen by the target to its own advantage.
- **Target strength itself.** A table for a class, not a measurement of this hull.

**The system**

- **Noise.** Modeled isotropic noise can be 10 dB from the truth on a given beam.
- **Detection threshold.** Depends on the operator and the clutter of the day.
- **Array condition.** Failed elements, fouling, flow noise.

:::key
The engine’s numerical error is usually the smallest item on this list. Most prediction error is input error, and most of that is the bottom, the layer and the target.
:::

## Sensitivity

Before asking how wrong a prediction might be, ask which inputs it is sensitive to. Sensitivity depends on the situation.

- In a **surface duct**, mixed layer depth and sea state dominate. Bottom type is irrelevant.
- In **shallow summer** water, bottom loss dominates. Sea state hardly matters.
- At a **convergence zone**, the deep profile and the depth excess decide whether the zone exists. Small changes move it by kilometers.
- When **reverberation limited**, scattering strength and the waveform dominate, and source level does not matter at all.

A sensitivity study changes one input at a time by its plausible error and records how much the answer moves. The inputs that move it most are the ones worth measuring.

How much a decibel is worth in range depends on the slope of the loss curve at the detection range. Where loss rises steeply, a 5 dB error moves the range slightly. Where loss is nearly flat, the same error can double or halve it.

:::operator
Flat loss curves are both the best and the most treacherous conditions. Ranges are long, and they are exquisitely sensitive to a few decibels. A long predicted range in a duct deserves more skepticism than a short one in summer shelf water.
:::

## Ensembles

A single prediction uses one value for every input. An **ensemble** runs the prediction many times, each time drawing the uncertain inputs from their plausible ranges. The result is a family of answers.

::play

From the family, read:

- the **median**, a central estimate less sensitive to outliers than a single run
- the **spread**, for example the 10th to 90th percentile, as an honest statement of doubt
- the **probability** of meeting some requirement, such as signal excess above zero at a given range

Ensembles change the kind of statement that can be made. Not “detection range is 18 km” but “detection range is between 11 and 24 km, most likely 18”.

They cost engine runs. If the perturbed inputs affect propagation, each member needs its own. This is the practical case for fast surrogate engines. If the perturbed inputs are only noise, target strength or threshold, the members are nearly free, since loss is reused.

:::try
Set every uncertainty to zero and the seventy curves collapse into one. Raise only the “loss shape” slider and see the curves slide sideways. Raise only “noise” and see them move up and down. Different kinds of doubt leave different signatures.
:::

# Measurement

## In situ profiles

The cheapest large improvement to a prediction is a measured sound speed profile at the sensor.

A dropped probe returns temperature against depth in a few minutes. Converted to sound speed, with salinity from climatology, it gives the real mixed layer depth, the real thermocline gradient and any surprises such as a shallow secondary duct.

What it changes:

- whether a surface duct exists and how deep
- the depth and severity of the shadow zone
- the best depth for a variable depth sensor

What it does not change:

- the profile 30 km away along the propagation path
- the deep ocean below the probe’s reach, which must be completed from climatology
- anything about the bottom

A measured profile is merged with climatology below its deepest point, and the join must be smooth. An artificial kink in sound speed at the join bends rays like a real layer.

:::pipeline
A new profile invalidates every loss field computed from the old one. It is the measurement that costs a rerun. Record the profile’s time and position with each product so that a display can show which predictions used it.
:::

## Assimilating beam noise and reverberation

Units 9 and 10 introduced measured beam noise and measured reverberation. Here is how they tighten a prediction.

**Measured beam noise** removes most of the doubt in the noise term. A modeled value uncertain by 5 to 10 dB is replaced by an observed value good to a decibel or two, with the true directionality. Noise-limited predictions improve at once, for that sensor and that time.

**Measured reverberation** does the same for the masking at short and middle ranges. It can also be used to **calibrate**: adjust the model’s scattering strength until model matches measurement, then use the corrected model for other depths and settings.

In the ensemble viewer, switch on each measurement and watch the spread narrow.

Limits to keep in view:

- Both measurements are for **this sensor, this processor, now**.
- Neither says anything about transmission loss to the target. They fix the masking, not the echo.
- Each must be brought to the same reference point as the rest of the equation.

:::key
Measurement fixes the terms on the masking side of the equation. The echo side, loss and target strength, remains a prediction. After assimilation, loss and target strength are what is left to be uncertain about.
:::

## Model against data

Comparing predictions with what actually happened is how a prediction system earns trust. There are levels to it.

**Term by term.** Compare modeled noise with measured beam noise, modeled reverberation with measured. These are available on every ping, need no target, and localize the error to one term.

**Loss.** Compare modeled transmission loss with measurements from a calibrated source at known range. This needs a dedicated experiment and is the gold standard for the engine and its environmental inputs.

**End to end.** Compare predicted detection ranges with ranges at which known targets were actually detected. This is the test that matters and the hardest to interpret, since every term and the operator are in it together.

A useful discipline is to record, for every prediction, what was forecast and what was then observed, and to look at the differences over many cases. A model that is 6 dB optimistic on average over soft bottoms has a correctable bias. One that is right on average with a 10 dB scatter has a different problem.

Researchers go further and invert the comparison, using measured reverberation or loss to estimate bottom parameters. This works, with the standing caveat that different bottoms can produce the same data.

# Validation and new methods

## Verification against benchmarks

Before a system’s predictions can be compared with the sea, its arithmetic has to be right. That is **verification**, and it uses cases with known answers.

- **Analytic cases.** Uniform water with a perfectly reflecting surface and bottom has an exact solution. So does a simple wedge. An engine must reproduce them.
- **Published benchmarks.** The community has standard problems for propagation and, from the Reverberation Modeling Workshops, for reverberation. For target strength, the BeTSSi benchmark submarine has been computed by many groups with different numerical methods, including recent work extending it to bistatic geometries.
- **Engine against engine.** Where a ray model and a parabolic equation model are both valid, they should agree to within a couple of decibels on incoherent loss.

Verification says the equations were solved correctly. It does not say they were the right equations for the ocean, or that the inputs were right. That is **validation**, and it needs data.

:::key
Verification: did we build the model right. Validation: did we build the right model. A system needs both, and passing the first says nothing about the second.
:::

## Regression testing the pipeline

Engines change slowly. The software around them changes weekly. Tests keep that change from altering answers by accident.

**Golden cases.** A small set of fixed inputs with stored outputs. Every build recomputes them and compares. Tolerances are in decibels: a tenth for arithmetic stages, perhaps half for an engine across compilers.

**Property tests.** The physics checks from unit 17, automated: reciprocity, spherical spreading, sign of loss, source level cancelling in reverberation, slice agreement.

**Unit tests on conversions.** Every angle convention, unit conversion and level reference crossing a boundary gets a test with a hand-computed value.

**Visual regression.** Render each standard display from a golden case and compare images with the stored versions. This catches color map, scale, legend and resampling changes that numeric tests on the data never see.

:::pipeline
Golden cases should include ugly ones: a shadow zone, a cell with no arrivals, a radial that runs aground, a measured input with a gap. Clean cases pass. The edge cases are where a refactor silently changes behavior.
:::

## Surrogates and machine learning

The newest development in propagation modeling is the learned **surrogate**: a neural network trained on many runs of a conventional engine that then returns a loss field in a fraction of the time.

Published approaches include:

- convolutional networks that map a description of the environment directly to a loss field
- networks that take ray trajectories as input and predict loss from them
- **neural operators**, which learn the mapping between functions and can be given physical structure, for example encodings drawn from the parabolic equation
- **probabilistic** surrogates that return an uncertainty with each prediction

The appeal is speed. A surrogate thousands of times faster than the engine makes large ensembles, interactive what-if exploration and on-board prediction practical.

The cautions are equally real.

- A surrogate knows only the environments it was trained on. Outside them it fails without warning, and its output still looks like a loss field.
- It inherits every limitation of the engine that taught it.
- Most produce loss only. Active prediction needs angles and times for reverberation and beam patterns.
- Certifying a learned model for operational use is an open problem.

:::operator
A sensible use today is as a fast first look and an ensemble generator, with the standard engine run for the answers that matter. Where a surrogate supplies a field, the display should say so.
:::
