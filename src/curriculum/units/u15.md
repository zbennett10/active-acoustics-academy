# The triangle

## Source, target, receiver

Move the receiver away from the source and one line becomes a triangle.

- The **baseline**, of length $L$, joins the source to the receiver.
- $R_T$ is the range from the source, the transmitter, to the target.
- $R_R$ is the range from the target to the receiver.
- The **bistatic angle** $\beta$ is the angle at the target between the direction to the source and the direction to the receiver.

When $\beta = 0$, source and receiver lie in the same direction from the target and the geometry is monostatic. When $\beta = 180°$, the target lies on the baseline between them.

The sound still makes two legs, out and back, but they are now different legs: different lengths, different paths through the ocean and different angles at the target.

::play

:::try
Drag the target along a wide arc around both sensors and watch the bistatic angle. It is small far away, where the pair looks like a single point, and opens up as the target approaches the baseline.
:::

:::key
Everything in bistatic sonar comes back to this triangle. Timing depends on the **sum** of the two ranges. Loss depends on their **product**. Target strength and Doppler depend on the **angle between them**.
:::

## Equal-time ellipses

The receiver times the echo. The sound has travelled $R_T + R_R$, so

$$
R_T + R_R = c\,t
$$

where $t$ is the time since transmission. All points for which the sum of distances to two fixed points is constant lie on an **ellipse** with those points as its foci. One echo time defines one ellipse. The target is somewhere on it.

To fix the position, add the bearing of the echo at the receiver. Where the bearing line crosses the ellipse is the target. With $\rho = R_T + R_R$ and $\theta$ the angle at the receiver between the direction to the source and the direction to the echo:

$$
R_R = \frac{\rho^2 - L^2}{2\,(\rho - L\cos\theta)}
$$

Receivers usually measure delay from the arrival of the **direct blast**, the transmission heard straight across the baseline. That arrives at $L/c$, so the echo follows it by $(\rho - L)/c$.

:::pipeline
Bistatic localization needs things a monostatic sonar never asks for: the position of a source that may be on another platform, the time it transmitted, and the baseline length. Errors in any of them move the ellipse. Position and time of every node are part of the data model.
:::

## Equal-loss ovals

Where is the echo strongest? With simple spherical spreading on both legs, the two-way loss is

$$
TL_{out} + TL_{back} = 20 \log R_T + 20 \log R_R = 20 \log (R_T R_R)
$$

So loss is constant where the **product** $R_T R_R$ is constant. Those curves are **ovals of Cassini**.

- For a small product they are two separate loops, one around the source and one around the receiver.
- At $R_T R_R = (L/2)^2$ the loops touch at the midpoint in a figure of eight.
- For larger products they merge into a single oval, pinched in the middle and then nearly round.

A convenient summary is the **equivalent monostatic range**, the range at which a monostatic sonar would suffer the same spreading loss:

$$
R_{eq} = \sqrt{R_T R_R}
$$

:::operator
The white zero signal excess line in the playground is one of these ovals. Pull the source and receiver apart and it pinches, then splits in two. A baseline that is too long leaves a gap in the middle.
:::

# What changes

## The direct blast and the blind zone

The receiver hears the transmission directly, across the baseline, before any echo. This **direct blast** is enormously strong, having suffered only one-way loss and no target. It saturates the receiver while it lasts.

An echo can be heard only after the direct blast has passed. The blast arrives at $L/c$ and lasts for the pulse length $\tau$. An echo whose total path is shorter than

$$
\rho_{blind} = L + c\,\tau
$$

arrives inside it and is lost.

That boundary is an ellipse too, a thin one hugging the baseline. Inside it is the **blind zone**. Its half-width at the middle of the baseline is about $\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{2Lc\tau}$ for short pulses: for a 20 km baseline and a 1 s pulse, close to 4 km on either side.

The blind zone contains the region of highest bistatic angle, including the forward scatter geometry of unit 8. The largest target strengths and the complete loss of the echo occur in the same place.

:::pitfall
A coverage map that leaves out the blind zone shows its best performance exactly on the baseline, where the product of ranges is smallest. In reality that strip is blank.
:::

## Bistatic target strength and aspect

The target is struck from one direction and observed from another. Two aspect angles are needed, and unit 8 gave the tools.

- For modest bistatic angles, use the monostatic target strength at the **bisector** aspect.
- Watch for **specular** geometry, where the hull’s long side mirrors the source toward the receiver.

::play{preset="aspect"}

With aspect-dependent target strength switched on, the signal excess field is no longer a smooth oval. Bright streaks cross it: places where a target on that heading would be at the mirror angle for this source and receiver.

The consequence is important. A bistatic pair can get a beam-aspect return from a target that is bow-on to the source, because the receiver is somewhere else.

:::try
Leave the three nodes where they are and turn the target’s heading slowly. The streaks rotate. For some headings the target sits on one and its target strength jumps by 10 dB or more.
:::

## Bistatic Doppler

The echo’s frequency shift depends on how fast the **total path** $R_T + R_R$ is shrinking. Each leg contributes the component of target velocity along it:

$$
\Delta f = \frac{f}{c}\left(v_T + v_R\right)
$$

where $v_T$ is the target’s speed toward the source and $v_R$ its speed toward the receiver. Written with the bistatic angle, for a target moving at speed $s$ in a direction making an angle $\delta$ with the bisector:

$$
\Delta f = \frac{2\,s\,f}{c}\,\cos\delta\,\cos\!\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)
$$

Three things to take from it.

- At $\beta = 0$ it is the monostatic formula.
- Doppler is greatest for motion along the **bisector** and zero for motion across it, which means along the equal-time ellipse.
- The factor $\cos(\beta/2)$ shrinks every Doppler shift as the angle opens. At $\beta = 120°$ shifts are halved. Near the baseline they vanish, and CW processing loses its grip.

:::operator
A target that has turned to give one sonar zero Doppler is probably not giving zero Doppler to a receiver elsewhere. Bistatic geometry makes it hard to hide from Doppler in two places at once.
:::

## Resolution and the bistatic angle

A waveform with bandwidth $B$ resolves total path to $c/B$. Across a monostatic geometry that is $c/2B$ in range. In a bistatic geometry the resolution cell, measured along the bisector, widens:

$$
\Delta r = \frac{c}{2B\,\cos(\beta/2)}
$$

At $\beta = 90°$ the cell is 41 percent deeper. At 150° it is nearly four times deeper.

A larger cell holds more reverberation. Localization accuracy degrades the same way, because the equal-time ellipses crowd together least in the direction of the bisector when the angle is large.

So large bistatic angles carry a double penalty apart from the blind zone: less Doppler and coarser range. The comfortable region for a bistatic pair is to the sides and beyond the ends of the baseline, where the angle is moderate.

# Predicting bistatic performance

## Two loss fields

A monostatic prediction needs one loss field: from the sensor to every point, used twice. A bistatic prediction needs two.

- $TL_{out}$: from the **source**, at the source’s depth, to the target.
- $TL_{back}$: from the target to the **receiver**, at the receiver’s depth.

By reciprocity the second is the same as the loss from the receiver to the target. So the practical recipe is to run the engine once centered on the source and once centered on the receiver, each over its own set of radials, then evaluate both at each target position:

$$
EL = SL - TL_{src \to tgt} - TL_{rcv \to tgt} + TS(\text{aspects})
$$

The source and receiver can be at different depths, in different water, even on opposite sides of a front. A source in the surface duct and a receiver below the layer is a legitimate and useful combination.

:::pipeline
The two fields are on different polar grids with different centers. Combining them means resampling both onto a common grid of target positions. That resampling, not the physics, is where most bistatic plumbing errors live.
:::

## Bistatic reverberation

Reverberation has the same geometry as the echo. At any instant, the scatterers contributing are those whose total path from source to receiver matches the elapsed time: they lie on the **equal-time ellipse** for that instant, within the receiver’s beam.

Compared with the monostatic case:

- The scattering is no longer straight back. The incoming and outgoing grazing angles differ, and the scattered direction can be well away from the backscatter direction.
- The contributing area is where the beam crosses the ellipse’s annulus, and its size depends on the angle between them.
- A receiver far from the source hears reverberation that has spread further on its second leg, and so is generally weaker.

That last point is a real advantage. The receiver can also be quieter, deeper and better placed than the source platform.

:::key
Full reverberation models compute both the monostatic and the bistatic case. CASS does. The inputs are the same as before, with the scattering strength now a function of two grazing angles and the horizontal scattering angle.
:::

## Why go bistatic

Separating the source from the receiver costs coordination. What it buys:

- **A silent receiver.** The receiving platform does not transmit and does not reveal itself.
- **A second aspect.** The target cannot present its bow to both.
- **Depth diversity.** Source and receiver can be on opposite sides of the layer.
- **A better receiver.** A large quiet array far from the noisy source platform.
- **One source, many receivers.** The step to multistatic, in the next unit.

What it costs:

- The **blind zone** along the baseline.
- **Timing and position** of the source must be known at the receiver.
- **Geometry-dependent** resolution, Doppler and target strength, none of which are constant over the search area.
- A prediction with **twice the propagation** and a much larger target strength lookup.

:::operator
A bistatic prediction should be read as a shape, not a range. The interesting questions are where the oval pinches, where the blind zone lies, and where the mirror streaks fall for the target headings you consider likely.
:::
