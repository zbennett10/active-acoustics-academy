# Time and range

## Echo time and range

In a monostatic sonar the source and receiver are together, so the sound travels to the target and back along the same path. If the echo returns a time $t$ after transmission, the range is

$$
r = \frac{c\,t}{2}
$$

The factor of two is the round trip. At 1500 m/s:

| Echo time | Range |
|---|---|
| 1 s | 750 m |
| 10 s | 7.5 km |
| 40 s | 30 km |
| 80 s | 60 km |

The time axis after a ping **is** a range axis. Everything the sonar records in one listening period, echo, reverberation, clutter, is laid out by range along it.

All points with the same echo time lie on a circle around the sensor. That simplicity is what monostatic geometry gives and what bistatic geometry takes away.

:::pipeline
Measured data from the sensor is in time. Predictions are in range. The conversion is one line, and it should use the same sound speed the prediction’s range grid assumes, or the two will not line up at long range.
:::

## Blind range and pulse length

A sonar that transmits and receives through the same array cannot listen while it is sending. An echo that returns before the pulse has finished is lost. That sets a **blind range**:

$$
r_{blind} = \frac{c\,\tau}{2}
$$

with $\tau$ the transmitted pulse length.

| Pulse length | Blind range |
|---|---|
| 50 ms | 38 m |
| 0.5 s | 375 m |
| 2 s | 1.5 km |
| 8 s | 6 km |

Note that it is the **transmitted** duration that matters here, not the compressed one. A long FM sweep has fine range resolution and is still blind while it is being sent.

In practice the blind range is somewhat larger, because the receiver needs time to recover from the transmission and the first moments are swamped by the strongest reverberation.

:::operator
Long pulses carry the energy needed for distant search and leave a hole around the ship. Sonars carry short pulses for exactly that hole.
:::

## Ping interval and the farthest range

After a ping the sonar listens, then pings again. The **ping interval**, $T_p$, limits how far it can look. An echo must return before the next transmission, or it will be taken for a close echo of that next ping:

$$
r_{max} = \frac{c\,T_p}{2}
$$

| Ping interval | Farthest unambiguous range |
|---|---|
| 10 s | 7.5 km |
| 30 s | 22.5 km |
| 90 s | 67.5 km |

A convergence zone at 60 km cannot be searched with a 30 s interval, whatever the signal excess says. The prediction and the ping schedule have to be considered together.

The interval also sets how often a target is seen. A long interval reaches far and reports slowly. Between pings a target at 20 knots moves 10 m every second, or 900 m in a 90 s interval.

:::pitfall
An echo from beyond $r_{max}$ does not vanish. It arrives during the next listening period and appears at a false short range. Strong returns from a distant convergence zone or seamount can show up as phantom close contacts.
:::

# Motion

## Doppler from range rate

If the distance between sonar and target is changing, the echo is shifted in frequency. For a closing speed $v$, the rate at which range is decreasing:

$$
\Delta f = \frac{2\,v\,f}{c}
$$

The factor of two appears because the motion compresses the wave once on the way out and again on the way back.

Only the component of velocity **along the line of sight** counts. A target crossing at right angles has no Doppler, whatever its speed. For a target moving at speed $s$ on a heading that makes an angle $\phi$ with the line toward the sonar:

$$
v = s \cos\phi
$$

At 3.5 kHz, each knot of closing speed gives 2.4 Hz. A 10-knot target heading straight in shifts the echo up by 24 Hz. The same target on a beam course gives nothing.

The sonar’s own motion adds its own shift to every echo and to the reverberation, different in each direction. Processors remove it by **own-Doppler nullification**, shifting each beam’s spectrum by the amount the ship’s speed predicts for that bearing. After that, zero Doppler means zero motion over the ground along that beam.

:::try
In the monostatic sweep, read the closing speed and Doppler in the corner while the target moves. Set its course at right angles to the bearing and watch both fall to zero as it passes its closest point.
:::

## Search rate and coverage

A moving sonar sweeps out area. How much depends on how wide its detection zone is and how fast the platform can go while keeping it.

For a detection radius $r_d$ and platform speed $u$, the area swept per unit time is roughly

$$
\text{sweep rate} \approx 2\,r_d\,u
$$

Both factors fight each other through self noise: going faster shrinks $r_d$ once flow noise takes over.

The ping interval adds a second constraint. Between pings the platform advances $u\,T_p$. If that exceeds the width of the detection zone, there are gaps along the track where nothing was ever pinged. For a thin convergence zone annulus the tolerance is small.

:::operator
Coverage is the detection zone combined with a ping schedule and a track. A wide zone searched with too long an interval at too high a speed leaves stripes of unsearched water.
:::

# The plan view

## From a loss curve to rings

A plan view looks down on the sensor from above. For a monostatic sonar in an ocean that is the same in all directions, turning a signal excess curve into a plan view is a rotation: every range where signal excess is positive becomes a ring.

::play

A typical deep-water picture has three parts:

- a filled disc around the sensor: the direct path
- a wide empty band
- a thin ring far out: the first convergence zone

Then the timing limits cut into it. The blind range removes a small disc at the center. The ping interval draws a circle beyond which nothing can be attributed correctly.

:::try
Let the target run inbound. It shows as contacts while it crosses the outer ring, disappears for many pings in the gap, and returns inside the direct path. Now shorten the ping interval until the dashed circle falls inside the outer ring, and watch those contacts stop.
:::

## Bearing dependence

Real plan views are not circles. Three things make them depend on bearing.

**The ocean changes with direction.** Bathymetry shoals one way and deepens another. A front lies to the north. Each bearing radial gets its own loss curve from its own slice of ocean, and its own set of rings. Joined around the compass they make an irregular shape.

**Noise changes with direction.** A beam toward shipping hears more. Detection range is shorter on those bearings even where the ocean is identical.

**The sensor is not symmetric.** A hull array is masked aft by the ship. A towed line array has broad beams toward its ends. Transmit and receive patterns vary with bearing.

:::pipeline
The plan view grid is polar: bearing by range. The bearings come from the engine’s radials. Between radials, values are interpolated. If the ocean has a feature narrower than the spacing between radials, the plan view will not contain it.
:::

## Monostatic strengths and limits

Monostatic operation is the baseline everything else is compared with.

**Strengths**

- One platform, no coordination, no shared timing.
- Simple geometry: range from time, bearing from beam.
- Reciprocity means one loss field serves both directions.
- No blind zone other than the small one around the sensor.

**Limits**

- The target is seen from **one** aspect. If that aspect is the bow, there is no second opinion.
- Reverberation is **backscatter**, straight back along the path, with the source’s full level behind it.
- The platform that pings is the platform that is heard. It gives away its own position.
- Source and receiver share one depth, or nearly. Both are on the same side of the layer.

Each limit is an argument for separating the source from the receiver. That is the next unit.

:::key
Monostatic is the simplest geometry and the least forgiving of an unlucky aspect. Bistatic and multistatic geometries exist to buy second looks.
:::
