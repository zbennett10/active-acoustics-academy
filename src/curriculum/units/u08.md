# Definition and simple shapes

## What target strength means

**Target strength**, TS, says how much of the sound that reaches a target comes back toward the receiver. It is defined by comparing two intensities:

$$
TS = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{I_{\text{echo at 1 m}}}{I_{\text{incident}}}\right)
$$

The echo intensity is referred to one meter from the target’s acoustic center, by the same convention that refers source level to one meter from a source. That lets the echo be treated as a new source at the target, with level $SL - TL + TS$, which then spreads back to the receiver.

Target strength is often **positive** for large objects. That does not mean the target amplifies sound. It is an artifact of the one-meter reference: a submarine is far larger than one meter, and the point one meter from its “center” lies inside it. The value is correct as bookkeeping and should not be read as a gain.

:::key
Target strength is a property of the target, the frequency, and the directions of arrival and departure. It does not depend on range or on the source level.
:::

## Simple shapes

A few shapes have closed-form answers when they are rigid and large compared with the wavelength. They are worth knowing because real targets are built from pieces that behave like them.

| Shape | Seen from | Target strength |
|---|---|---|
| Sphere, radius $a$ | Anywhere | $10 \log (a^2/4)$ |
| Cylinder, radius $a$, length $L$ | Broadside | $10 \log (aL^2/2\lambda)$ |
| Flat plate, area $A$ | Face on | $20 \log (A/\lambda)$ |

Some numbers:

- A sphere of radius 2 m has a target strength of exactly 0 dB, at any frequency high enough for the rule to hold.
- A cylinder 4 m in radius and 15 m long, at 3.5 kHz, gives $10 \log (4 \times 225 / 0.86) = 30$ dB.
- A flat plate of 10 m² at the same frequency gives $20 \log(10/0.43) = 27$ dB.

Notice the pattern. A sphere scatters in all directions and does not depend on wavelength. Flat and straight surfaces return sound like mirrors, strongly and only in one direction, and more strongly as the wavelength shortens.

:::math
The large returns from cylinders and plates come with a narrow angular width, about $\lambda/L$ radians. A 15 m section at 3.5 kHz reflects its full strength over only about 1.6°. Outside that it falls quickly.
:::

## Size against wavelength

How a body scatters depends on its size compared with the wavelength, usually expressed as $ka$, where $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ and $a$ is a typical dimension.

- **Small, $ka \ll 1$: Rayleigh scattering.** The body is too small to form a reflection. It scatters weakly, and the strength climbs as the fourth power of frequency, 12 dB per octave.
- **Comparable, $ka \sim 1$ to 10: resonance.** Waves creep around the body and interfere with the direct reflection. Target strength oscillates with frequency.
- **Large, $ka \gg 1$: geometric.** The body reflects like an optical object and the table in the previous leaf applies.

At 3.5 kHz, $k$ is about 15 per meter. A submarine hull is geometric. A fish’s swim bladder a few centimeters across sits near resonance, which is why small animals can scatter far more than their size suggests.

:::operator
Sonar frequency is chosen partly with this in mind. Low frequencies travel far but see small objects poorly. High frequencies see detail and do not travel.
:::

# Real targets

## Aspect dependence

A real hull is not a sphere. Its target strength depends strongly on the direction it is viewed from.

::play

The pattern has a classic shape, sometimes called a butterfly:

- **Beam aspect** gives the highest values, where the long, nearly parallel side of the hull faces the sonar. Textbook nominal figures for a submarine are around 25 dB.
- **Bow and stern** give the lowest, around 10 dB, since the hull presents little flat area.
- In between, lobes and nulls come from individual features and from tapered sections that face slightly forward or aft.

Within the beam lobe there is often a much narrower spike: the mirror reflection from the straight midbody, only a degree or two wide.

:::try
Drag the sonar slowly through 90°. The broad wing changes by a few decibels over tens of degrees. The spike at exactly 90° comes and goes within two. A prediction that assumed “beam aspect, 25 dB” was describing the wing, not the spike.
:::

:::pitfall
Fifteen decibels between beam and bow is the difference between detection at twice the range and none at all. A single target strength in a prediction is an assumption about what the target will choose to do.
:::

## Highlights and echo structure

At sonar frequencies the echo from a large target is not one reflection. It is the sum of several **highlights**: the bow, the sail, the stern planes, changes in hull section. Each returns its own small echo at its own delay.

Two things follow.

**The echo is stretched.** A target of length $L$ seen at an angle $\theta$ off the bow spreads its highlights over a range extent of $L\,|\cos\theta|$. A 60 m hull seen bow-on returns an echo 60 m deep in range, or 80 ms long.

**Target strength depends on the pulse.** A long pulse covers the whole target at once and all highlights add. A pulse shorter than the target resolves the highlights, and any one range cell holds only part of the total. The peak target strength seen by a high-resolution processor is lower than the value for a long pulse.

:::pipeline
This is a quiet per-processor effect. A wideband FM processor with 2 m resolution and a long CW processor do not see the same target strength for the same target. If one table serves both, state which pulse it describes.
:::

## Frequency, D/E and coatings

Three more things move target strength.

**Frequency.** Mirror-like returns strengthen with frequency and narrow at the same time, so the peak rises while the chance of catching it falls. Averaged over a few degrees of aspect, target strength in the geometric regime changes only slowly with frequency.

**D/E.** The vertical angle matters as much as the horizontal one. Features that reflect from the side may present nothing from below. Seen from directly above or below, a hull shows its largest flat area and can return a very strong, very narrow echo.

**Coatings.** Rubber tiles on a hull absorb sound and decouple the hull from the water. They work over a band of frequencies set by their thickness and construction, and are less effective at low frequency, where the wavelength is long compared with the tile.

:::key
Published nominal values such as 25 dB at the beam and 10 dB at the bow are historical averages for uncoated hulls. They are a starting point for learning the equation, not data about any real vessel.
:::

## Nominal values and tables

Urick’s textbook gives a table of nominal target strengths that is still quoted everywhere:

| Target | Aspect | Nominal TS |
|---|---|---|
| Submarine | Beam | +25 dB |
| Submarine | Bow or stern | +10 dB |
| Surface ship | Beam | +25 dB |
| Surface ship | Off beam | +15 dB |
| Mine | Beam | +10 dB |
| Torpedo | Bow | −20 dB |
| Large whale | Dorsal | about +5 dB |
| Fish, 30 cm | Dorsal | about −30 dB |

Prediction systems carry something richer: a table of target strength indexed by

- **aspect**, usually every few degrees
- **D/E**
- **frequency** or band
- and for bistatic work, a **second aspect and D/E** for the receiver

:::pipeline
Check three conventions in any target strength table: where aspect zero is, which way it increases, and whether the table is for the direction sound comes **from** or goes **to**. A table read backwards swaps bow for stern.
:::

# Bistatic target strength

## Two directions instead of one

In a monostatic geometry, sound arrives and leaves along the same line, so one aspect describes it. In a bistatic geometry the source and receiver see the target from different directions, and target strength depends on both.

::play{preset="bistatic"}

The angle between the two directions, measured at the target, is the **bistatic angle** $\beta$. At $\beta = 0$ the geometry is monostatic.

For small bistatic angles a useful rule applies, the bistatic equivalence theorem: the bistatic target strength is close to the **monostatic** value seen from the **bisector** of the two directions. If the source is at aspect 70° and the receiver at 110°, treat it as a monostatic look at 90°, the beam.

The rule degrades as the bistatic angle opens and should not be trusted beyond roughly a right angle.

:::try
The map on the right shows every combination of source and receiver aspect. The dashed diagonal is the monostatic case. Drag across the map and find the bright lines that do **not** lie on that diagonal.
:::

## Specular glint and forward scatter

The bright lines off the diagonal are two different effects.

**Specular glint.** A flat or straight surface reflects like a mirror: the angle of reflection equals the angle of arrival. For the hull’s long side, a source at 60° sends its mirror reflection toward 120°. A receiver sitting there sees the full beam-aspect strength even though neither the source nor the receiver is on the beam. As the target turns, the glint sweeps past like sunlight off a window.

**Forward scatter.** When the receiver is directly behind the target as seen from the source, $\beta$ near 180°, it sits in the target’s acoustic shadow. By Babinet’s principle the shadow is equivalent to a strong wave radiated forward, with

$$
TS_{forward} \approx 20 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{A}{\lambda}\right)
$$

where $A$ is the target’s silhouette area. For a 60 m by 8 m hull at 3.5 kHz that is $20 \log (480/0.43) = 61$ dB, far above any backscatter value.

:::pitfall
Forward scatter is rarely usable. The echo arrives at almost the same time and from almost the same direction as the direct blast from the source, which is tens of decibels stronger still. The geometry with the highest target strength is also the one inside the blind zone.
:::

## Target strength in the pipeline

Target strength is the hardest term to pin down, and the reasons are structural.

- **Aspect is unknown.** It depends on the target’s heading, which the prediction has to assume. Common choices are beam, bow, or an average over all aspects. Each gives a different answer by ten decibels or more.
- **The lookup needs angles from the engine.** Aspect comes from geometry. D/E at the target comes from the eigenrays. If loss was reduced to a single number per cell, the D/E has been lost.
- **Bistatic tables are large.** Two aspects, two D/E angles and frequency make a five-dimensional lookup. Many systems fall back to the bisector rule with a monostatic table.
- **It varies ping to ping.** Highlights interfere, and small changes in aspect swing the total by several decibels. This fluctuation is one reason signal excess is converted to a probability and not treated as a yes or no.

:::operator
When a display offers a choice of target aspect, use it. Looking at the coverage for beam and for bow side by side shows how much of the picture is the ocean and how much is a guess about the target.
:::
