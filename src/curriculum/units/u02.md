# Ratios

## Why decibels

Sound in the sea spans an absurd range. The intensity of a ping one meter from the source can exceed the intensity of its echo by a factor of $10^{16}$ or more. Nobody wants to carry sixteen zeros through a calculation.

The decibel solves two problems at once. It compresses that range into numbers between 0 and 250, and it turns multiplication into addition. A loss that divides intensity by a thousand becomes “subtract 30”.

A decibel always expresses a **ratio of two powers**:

$$
L = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P}{P_{ref}}\right)\ \text{dB}
$$

Read that definition literally. A decibel value is meaningless until you know what it is a ratio *to*.

:::key
A number of decibels is a ratio. A **level** is a ratio to an agreed reference, and it must name that reference.
:::

## Power and pressure

Hydrophones measure pressure. Intensity, the power crossing a unit area, goes as pressure squared. So a ratio of pressures becomes decibels with a factor of 20:

$$
L = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{p^2}{p_{ref}^2}\right) = 20 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{p}{p_{ref}}\right)
$$

Both forms give the same number of decibels for the same physical change. The factor differs only because pressure and power are different quantities.

| Change | Power ratio | Pressure ratio |
|---|---|---|
| +3 dB | × 2 | × 1.41 |
| +6 dB | × 4 | × 2 |
| +10 dB | × 10 | × 3.16 |
| +20 dB | × 100 | × 10 |
| −10 dB | × 0.1 | × 0.316 |

:::pitfall
Using $10 \log$ on a pressure ratio, or $20 \log$ on a power ratio, is the most common decibel error in code. Check what the variable holds before choosing the factor. Spreading area, bandwidth, time and number of elements are power-like: use 10. Pressure, voltage and amplitude use 20.
:::

## Mental arithmetic

Two facts handle almost every conversion in your head:

- **10 dB is a factor of ten.**
- **3 dB is a factor of two.**

Break any value into tens and threes.

- 37 dB = 30 + 7 = 30 + 10 − 3. That is × 1000 × 10 ÷ 2 = **× 5000**.
- 16 dB = 10 + 6. That is × 10 × 4 = **× 40**.
- −13 dB = −10 − 3. That is ÷ 10 ÷ 2 = **÷ 20**.

A few more anchors are worth knowing by heart: 1 dB is about 26 percent more power, 5 dB is close to a factor of 3, and 7 dB is a factor of 5.

# Levels and references

## A level needs a reference

Underwater acoustics uses a reference pressure of **one micropascal**. The common references are:

| Quantity | Written as | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Sound pressure level | dB re 1 µPa | Pressure at a point |
| Source level | dB re 1 µPa at 1 m | Level the source would produce one meter away |
| Spectrum level | dB re 1 µPa²/Hz | Level in a band one hertz wide |
| Transmission loss | dB re 1 m | Ratio of level at 1 m to level at the field point |
| Target strength | dB re 1 m² | Ratio of echo at 1 m to incident level |

Under the terminology standard ISO 18405, source level is formally written re 1 µPa²·m². It is the same quantity. The older “at 1 m” wording is still what you will see in most documents.

Air acoustics uses 20 µPa, so the same pressure reads 26 dB higher in water than in air before anything else is considered. Levels in air and levels in water cannot be compared by their numbers.

:::pipeline
Store the reference with the value. A field named `level_db` is a bug waiting for a second developer. `sl_db_re_1upa_1m`, or a units attribute on the array, is not.
:::

## Spectrum level and band level

Noise is spread over frequency. A **spectrum level** says how much lies in each one-hertz slice. A processor does not listen to one hertz. It listens to its whole band, and it collects the noise in all of it.

For a spectrum that is flat across a band of width $B$ hertz:

$$
L_{band} = L_{spectrum} + 10 \log_{10} B
$$

A spectrum level of 60 dB re 1 µPa²/Hz across a 400 Hz band gives $60 + 26 = 86$ dB re 1 µPa in the band.

| Bandwidth | $10 \log B$ |
|---|---|
| 1 Hz | 0 dB |
| 10 Hz | 10 dB |
| 100 Hz | 20 dB |
| 400 Hz | 26 dB |
| 1000 Hz | 30 dB |

:::pitfall
A spectrum level and a band level differ by tens of decibels and carry different units. Comparing one with the other is the second most common decibel error, after 10 versus 20. When a noise value arrives, the first question is whether it is per hertz.
:::

## Sign conventions for loss and gain

Transmission loss is quoted as a **positive** number of decibels. A loss of 80 dB means the level fell by 80. In the sonar equation it is subtracted:

$$
EL = SL - TL_{out} - TL_{back} + TS
$$

Gains are positive too, and they are added on the signal side or subtracted on the noise side. Directivity index is subtracted from noise. Processing gain is either added to the signal-to-noise ratio or folded into a smaller detection threshold.

Some engines and file formats store the same physics with the opposite sign: a “propagation factor” or “pressure in dB” that is negative and grows more negative with range. It is the same field multiplied by minus one.

:::pitfall
Subtracting a value that is already negative adds loss back as gain. The symptom is an echo level that *increases* with range. Test for it: transmission loss must be positive and must generally grow as range grows.
:::

# Combining levels

## Adding levels

Two sources of 60 dB each do not make 120 dB. Levels are logarithms, and the things that add physically are powers. Convert to power, add, convert back:

$$
L_{total} = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(10^{L_1/10} + 10^{L_2/10} + \dots\right)
$$

This is the **power sum**, written $L_1 \oplus L_2$. Its behavior is worth knowing without a calculator.

| Difference between the two | Add to the larger |
|---|---|
| 0 dB | 3.0 dB |
| 1 dB | 2.5 dB |
| 3 dB | 1.8 dB |
| 6 dB | 1.0 dB |
| 10 dB | 0.4 dB |
| 20 dB | 0.04 dB |

So two equal levels add 3 dB, $N$ equal levels add $10 \log N$, and a contributor 10 dB down hardly matters.

:::key
The larger level dominates. Once reverberation is 10 dB above noise, noise can be ignored, and the other way round. Where they are equal, the masking is 3 dB above either.
:::

## Subtracting and averaging levels

The same rule runs backwards. A measurement of reverberation always includes noise:

$$
L_{measured} = RL \oplus N
$$

To recover the reverberation alone, subtract in power:

$$
RL = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(10^{L_{measured}/10} - 10^{N/10}\right)
$$

This only works while the measurement is clearly above the noise. Within about a decibel of the noise floor the subtraction is unstable, and the honest answer is that the reverberation is at or below the noise.

Averaging has the same trap. The average of the decibel values is not the level of the average power. For 60 dB and 80 dB:

- mean of the decibels: **70 dB**
- level of the mean power: **77 dB**

:::pipeline
Decide which average a product means and say so in its definition. Averaging over frequency inside a band and combining paths are power averages. Averaging decibels is occasionally right, for example when smoothing a display, and it always gives the lower number.
:::

## Decibels in the pipeline

A short list of the ways decibel data goes wrong between an engine and a display.

- **No energy is not zero decibels.** A cell no ray reached has a power of zero, which is minus infinity in decibels. Keep it distinct from missing data, which is no value at all. Shadow zones are real results.
- **Interpolation.** Interpolating a field in decibels is not the same as interpolating in power. Near a sharp shadow edge both are wrong in different ways. Know which one your resampler does.
- **Precision.** Decibel values need a tenth of a decibel at most. The underlying powers span many orders of magnitude and should stay in floating point until the last step.
- **Clipping.** A field clipped to a maximum loss of 120 dB looks tidy and hides how deep a shadow is. Clip for display, never in storage.
- **Units in transit.** Every array of levels should say what it is: its reference, whether it is per hertz, and whether loss is positive.

:::
