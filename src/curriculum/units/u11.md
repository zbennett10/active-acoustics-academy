# Decisions under noise

## Detection as a decision

Every sonar display hides a decision made thousands of times a second. For each cell of range, bearing and Doppler, the processor produces a number. The question is whether that number means a target is there.

There are two possible truths and two possible calls, which gives four outcomes.

| | Call: target | Call: nothing |
|---|---|---|
| **Target present** | Detection | Miss |
| **Target absent** | False alarm | Correct rejection |

The decision is made by comparing the output with a **threshold**. Above it, call a target. Below it, call nothing.

Noise makes this imperfect. Even with no target the output fluctuates, and sometimes it crosses the threshold by chance. Even with a target, the output sometimes dips below. No threshold can remove both kinds of error. It can only trade one for the other.

:::key
A detector is described by two probabilities. **Pd**, the probability of detection, is the chance of calling a target that is there. **Pfa**, the probability of false alarm, is the chance of calling one that is not.
:::

## Pd, Pfa and the threshold

Picture the processor’s output as a random number. With noise alone it scatters around some low value. With an echo present it scatters around a higher one. The two distributions overlap.

::play

The threshold is a vertical line across both.

- **Pfa** is the area of the noise-only distribution to the right of the line.
- **Pd** is the area of the echo-plus-noise distribution to the right of the line.

Move the threshold right and both areas shrink: fewer false alarms and fewer detections. Move it left and both grow.

The only way to get more of one without more of the other is to pull the distributions apart, which means more signal relative to noise. That separation is the whole purpose of everything upstream: source level, array gain and processing gain all exist to move the echo distribution away from the noise distribution.

:::try
Drag the threshold until false alarms in the stream below nearly vanish, and count the echoes you now miss. Then raise the detection index by 6 dB and find a threshold that gives both few false alarms and few misses.
:::

## The ROC curve

For a given signal-to-noise ratio, sliding the threshold from high to low traces a curve of Pd against Pfa. This is the **receiver operating characteristic**, the ROC curve.

Each signal-to-noise ratio has its own curve. Higher ratios bow further toward the top left corner, where detection is certain and false alarms never occur.

A sonar operates at one point on one curve. Choosing that point is a judgment about costs.

- A false alarm costs an operator’s attention, or a weapon, or a diverted ship.
- A miss costs the detection.

Active sonars choose very low false alarm probabilities, often $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-6}$ per cell. The reason is the number of cells. One ping examined over 100 beams, 5000 range cells and 20 Doppler bins makes ten million decisions. At a Pfa of $10^{-4}$ that is a thousand false alarms per ping.

:::operator
False alarm **rate**, what the operator experiences, is false alarm **probability** times the number of cells examined. A processor with finer resolution has more cells and needs a lower Pfa per cell to show the same clutter on the screen.
:::

# How much signal is enough

## Detection index

The separation between the two distributions is measured by the **detection index**, $d$. For the simplest case, two bell curves of equal width $\sigma$ whose means differ by $\Delta m$:

$$
d = \left(\frac{\Delta m}{\sigma}\right)^2
$$

The detection index is a signal-to-noise ratio **at the output of the processor**, where the decision is made.

Given a required Pd and Pfa, the detection index needed is fixed:

$$
d = \left[\,Q^{-1}(P_{fa}) - Q^{-1}(P_d)\,\right]^2
$$

where $Q^{-1}$ gives the number of standard deviations whose upper tail has that probability.

| Pd | Pfa | $d$ | $10 \log d$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | $10^{-2}$ | 5.4 | 7.3 dB |
| 0.5 | $10^{-4}$ | 13.8 | 11.4 dB |
| 0.5 | $10^{-6}$ | 22.6 | 13.5 dB |
| 0.9 | $10^{-4}$ | 25.0 | 14.0 dB |

Two observations. Tightening Pfa by a factor of 100 costs only 2 to 4 dB. And 50 percent detection is the cheapest target to state, which is one reason it is the convention.

## Detection threshold

**Detection threshold**, DT, is the signal-to-noise ratio required at a chosen reference point in the receiver for the detection to succeed with the stated Pd and Pfa.

The detection index says what is needed at the output. DT translates that back to the input, where the echo and noise levels of the sonar equation are defined. The translation depends on the processor.

For a filter matched to a known pulse of duration $T$, with noise measured in a one-hertz band, Urick gives:

$$
DT = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d}{2T}\right)
$$

For $d = 13.8$ and a one-second pulse, $DT = 10 \log 6.9 = 8.4$ dB. A four-second pulse needs 6 dB less.

DT can be **negative**. A long pulse or a wide band lets a processor pull out an echo that lies below the noise at its input.

:::pitfall
A DT value means nothing without its reference. It may be relative to noise in a one-hertz band or in the processor’s band. It may include the processing gain or assume it is carried as a separate term. The same processor can honestly be described as having a DT of +10 dB or −16 dB. Find out which convention a table uses before putting it into an equation.
:::

## Recognition differential and the operator

The detection threshold of theory assumes an ideal decision maker. Real detections are made by people watching displays, or by automatic detectors tuned for an acceptable alert rate.

**Recognition differential**, RD, is the practical counterpart: the signal-to-noise ratio at which a trained operator, using a particular display, detects a contact half the time. It is determined by measurement and experience, and it folds in:

- how the display integrates over pings and across cells
- the operator’s alertness and training
- the clutter and false alarm load actually present

In prediction systems RD takes the place of DT in the sonar equation. It belongs to a **processor and display mode**. A sensor with several processors has several recognition differentials.

:::pipeline
The DT or RD used in a prediction is one of the most influential inputs and one of the least visible. It is a single number per processor, usually stored in a configuration table, and every decibel of it moves signal excess by a decibel everywhere.
:::

## False alarms at scale

A prediction says where detection is likely. It says nothing about how many false contacts will appear alongside. Yet false alarms decide whether a system is usable.

The standard defenses all trade a little detection for a large cut in false alarms.

- **M of N.** Require a contact to appear on at least $M$ of the last $N$ pings near the same place. Noise crossings rarely repeat. Targets do.
- **Tracking.** Require contacts to move in a physically plausible way before promoting them.
- **Normalization.** Estimate the background around each cell and threshold on the ratio, so that the false alarm rate stays constant as reverberation rises and falls. Unit 12 returns to this.
- **Classification.** Use echo shape, Doppler and extent to tell targets from clutter.

:::key
Signal excess describes one look at one cell. A detection the operator can act on is the product of several looks and a filter against false alarms. Keep the two ideas apart.
:::

# From signal excess to probability

## Signal excess and the 50 percent rule

**Signal excess** is how far the actual signal-to-noise ratio exceeds the detection threshold:

$$
SE = SNR - DT
$$

By construction, DT is the ratio at which detection probability is 50 percent. So:

- $SE = 0$: detection is a coin flip.
- $SE > 0$: more likely than not.
- $SE < 0$: less likely than not, and still not impossible.

This is the most misread number in sonar prediction. A contour of zero signal excess is not an edge beyond which nothing can be detected. It is the line where the odds are even.

:::operator
Describe a zero signal excess contour as “the 50 percent line”. Calling it “detection range” invites people to treat it as a wall.
:::

## Fluctuation and sigma

If everything were known exactly, detection probability would jump from near 0 to near 1 as signal excess crossed zero. It does not, because the real signal-to-noise ratio wanders around its predicted value.

- Target strength swings with small changes in aspect.
- Transmission loss fluctuates with internal waves and surface motion.
- Noise and reverberation vary from moment to moment.

These are treated together as a random error in signal excess, with standard deviation $\sigma$ in decibels, and the probability of detection follows:

$$
P_d = \Phi\!\left(\frac{SE}{\sigma}\right)
$$

where $\Phi$ is the cumulative normal distribution. Values of $\sigma$ between 6 and 10 dB are typical.

| Signal excess | Pd with σ = 8 dB |
|---|---|
| −16 dB | 2 % |
| −8 dB | 16 % |
| 0 dB | 50 % |
| +8 dB | 84 % |
| +16 dB | 98 % |

:::pipeline
$\sigma$ is an input, and often a hard-coded one. It sets how sharp or soft every probability map looks. A display that shows probability should be able to say what $\sigma$ was used.
:::

## Cumulative detection

One ping gives one chance. A target that stays in the area gets pinged many times, and the chance of at least one detection grows.

If the looks are independent, the probability of **at least one** detection in $n$ looks is

$$
P_{cum} = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{n}(1 - P_i)
$$

Five looks at 30 percent each give $1 - 0.7^5 = 83$ percent.

The catch is the word independent. Successive pings a few seconds apart see nearly the same ocean, the same target aspect and the same noise. If the first missed because the target was bow-on, the second will probably miss for the same reason. Treating them as independent overstates cumulative detection.

The same formula combines **different sensors** or **different processors** looking at one target. Independence is more believable there, since the paths and aspects differ. Unit 16 uses it across multistatic pairs.

:::pitfall
Cumulative probability from the independent-looks formula is an upper bound when the looks are correlated. The truth lies between it and the single best look.
:::
