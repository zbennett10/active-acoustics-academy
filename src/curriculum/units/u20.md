# Method

## A method for any scenario

Every scenario in this unit yields to the same sequence. Use it in order, and say each step aloud until it is habit.

1. **Read the water.** What does the profile do? Is there a layer, a duct, depth excess? What is the bottom, and will sound touch it?
2. **Find the paths.** Which path families connect the sensor to the depths a target might use? Where are the shadows?
3. **Choose the geometry.** Which sensor depth puts the sensor on a path to the target? Monostatic, or is there a reason to separate source and receiver?
4. **Name the limit.** At the ranges that matter, is the sonar limited by noise or by reverberation?
5. **Choose the processor.** If noise: energy. If reverberation: resolution or Doppler. What is the target likely to be doing?
6. **Work the budget.** Echo level, masking, threshold, signal excess. Where are the zones?
7. **State the doubt.** Which input is least certain, and how much would it move the answer? What measurement would settle it?

The order matters. Steps 1 to 3 are about the ocean and they decide most of the outcome. Steps 4 and 5 are about the sonar. Steps 6 and 7 are about the answer.

:::key
Most prediction mistakes are made by starting at step 6. The budget is easy. Knowing which loss curve to put in it is the skill.
:::

## Diagnosing a surprising display

When a prediction looks wrong, work backward through the pipeline. The shape of the surprise points to the stage.

| What you see | Look at |
|---|---|
| Everything too good or too bad by a constant | Source level, threshold, a missed or doubled gain term |
| Too good by about the directivity index | Measured beam noise with DI subtracted again |
| Too good by the processing gain | Threshold convention mixed between tables |
| Echo rises with range | Loss with the wrong sign |
| Range off by a factor of two | One-way time or loss used as two-way |
| Picture rotated | Beam to bearing without heading, or relative shown as true |
| Picture mirrored top to bottom | D/E sign convention |
| Bow and stern swapped | Aspect convention in the target table |
| Perfectly circular plan view | Isotropic noise and a range-independent ocean: correct, or missing radials |
| Smooth where a shadow should be | Interpolation across a gap, too few radials |
| One spoke differs from its neighbors | A bad profile or bathymetry sample on one radial |
| No change when source level changes | Reverberation limited: this one is physics |

:::operator
Change one input and predict the effect before looking. If the display does what you predicted, you understand it. If not, you have just located the problem to within one stage.
:::

# Scenarios

## The layer

**Tasking.** Open ocean, a mixed layer 100 m deep. A ship has a hull sonar at 40 m and a variable depth sonar that can go to 250 m. The target may stay shallow or go below the layer.

::play

**Read the water.** A positive gradient in the top 100 m makes a surface duct. Below it the thermocline bends sound down.

**Find the paths.** From 40 m, shallow-angle energy is trapped in the duct and reaches far along the surface. Steeper energy dives. Between them, below the layer, is a shadow zone starting a few kilometers out.

**Choose the geometry.** There are four combinations of sensor above or below and target above or below. Same side of the layer: good. Opposite sides: poor beyond short range.

**The lesson.** No single sensor depth covers both target depths. A ship with both sonars uses both. A ship with one must decide which target behavior it fears more.

:::

## Convergence zone search

**Tasking.** A deep basin, 5000 m, in mid latitudes. You need detections well beyond the direct path.

**Read the water.** A deep sound channel with its axis near 1300 m. Sound speed at the seabed exceeds sound speed at the surface, so there is depth excess.

**Find the paths.** Rays leaving the near-surface region downward turn deep and come back up together around 60 km. Between the end of the direct path and that zone there is very little.

**Work the budget.** The zone gives several decibels of gain over spherical spreading, and loss there is still near 90 dB one way. The figure of merit must reach roughly twice that. It is a noise-limited detection, so source level, array gain and a long pulse all help.

**Mind the timing.** A target at 60 km returns its echo after 80 s. The ping interval must be longer than that.

**State the doubt.** The zone’s range depends on the whole profile and moves by kilometers with season and position. Its width is a few kilometers. A plan built around the zone needs the profile to be right.

:::operator
A convergence zone detection gives a range ring, not a position, and the target crosses it quickly. It is a cue for another sensor more than a track in itself.
:::

## Summer shelf

**Tasking.** Continental shelf, 200 m of water, sand bottom, a strong summer thermocline at 20 to 60 m.

**Read the water.** Warm over cool: sound speed falls with depth. Everything bends down.

**Find the paths.** Every path is bottom bounce. Rays strike the seabed at grazing angles of several degrees and upward. Below the critical angle of sand, loss per bounce is small. The steeper ones do not last.

**Name the limit.** Reverberation, emphatically. The whole path is bottom interaction, and the bottom is close.

**Choose the processor.** Source level will not help. A wide FM sweep cuts the range cell and gains tens of decibels against reverberation. A CW pulse helps only if the target has Doppler.

**Choose the geometry.** A sensor below the thermocline reaches a deep target along paths that never climb into the warm layer. A target above the thermocline, in the warm surface layer, is hard to reach from below and from above alike.

**State the doubt.** The bottom. A patch of mud in a region charted as sand changes the answer by more than any setting on the sonar.

## Winter shelf

**Tasking.** The same shelf in winter. The water column is mixed. Wind is 25 knots.

**Read the water.** Uniform temperature. Sound speed rises gently with depth from pressure alone, so rays bend slightly up.

**Find the paths.** Sound is carried along by repeated surface reflection, with shallow-angle bottom contact. Bottom loss matters much less than in summer. Propagation is far better.

**What the wind does.** It acts three times: more surface loss on every bounce, more surface reverberation, and 10 to 15 dB more ambient noise than a calm day.

**Name the limit.** Noise at long range, surface reverberation closer in.

**Choose the geometry.** Depth matters little. There is no layer to be on the wrong side of.

**The lesson.** Summer and winter on the same shelf are different oceans. The summer problem is the bottom and is solved by resolution. The winter problem is the surface and the weather, and is partly solved by waiting.

In the scenario room, compare scenarios 3 and 4 with the same choices. Read the noise at the beam in each, then the detectable range.
:::

## Up the slope

**Tasking.** You are in deep water. The target is inshore, on the shelf, at 50 to 120 m. You are looking up a continental slope.

**Read the water.** Range dependent. The bottom rises from 1500 m to under 200 m over 30 km.

**Find the paths.** Sound heading inshore strikes the slope. Each reflection steepens the ray by twice the slope angle. After a few bounces the grazing angle passes the critical angle and the energy enters the seabed.

**What the full-field shows.** Good levels over the deep water, then a rapid falloff as the wedge of water narrows. Loss onto the shelf is far greater than either the deep-water or the shelf profile alone would suggest.

**Choose the geometry.** Looking upslope from deep water is the hard direction. A sensor on the shelf looking along it, or a bistatic receiver already inshore, avoids the slope entirely.

**State the doubt.** The bathymetry along the specific bearing. A canyon or a spur changes the answer, and it has to lie on a radial to be seen.

:::pitfall
A range-independent prediction made with the offshore profile and depth would show cheerful coverage across the shelf. This is the case that range dependence exists for.
:::

## A multistatic barrier

**Tasking.** Hold a line 60 km long against a target that must cross it. You have two sources and six receivers.

**Choose the geometry.** Open the field planner and load the barrier layout. Each source pairs with six receivers: twelve pairs.

**Find the gaps.** Along each baseline is a blind strip. Between nodes the ovals pinch. A gap that runs through the line from one side to the other is a path for the target.

**Use aspect.** A target crossing the line at right angles presents its beam to pairs whose bisector lies along the line, and its bow to pairs looking along its track. With nodes spread along the line, some pair is always near the mirror angle.

**Use depth.** If a layer is present, alternate receivers above and below it.

**Work the schedule.** Two sources pinging in turn halves the update rate from each. Pinging together requires distinguishable waveforms.

**State the doubt.** Run the layout for the worst target depth and for bow aspect. If the line still holds, it holds.

# Speaking the result

## Briefing a prediction

A prediction is finished when someone who has to act on it understands it. Four sentences usually do.

1. **The answer, with its shape.** “Against a target at 100 m, expect detection out to about 12 km, a gap, then a narrow zone near 58 km.”
2. **What it assumes.** “That is for beam aspect, with this morning’s profile and measured noise from 14:00.”
3. **What would change it most.** “If the target goes below 150 m, the first zone shrinks to 4 km.”
4. **How sure.** “The bottom is the weak point. A softer bottom than charted takes the bounce path away.”

Avoid a bare range. Avoid the word “will”. Avoid describing a 50 percent contour as a limit.

:::operator
The most useful thing a prediction can tell a commander is often negative: where the sensor cannot see. Lead with coverage and follow at once with the gaps.
:::

## Where to go next

You have worked from one ping to a multistatic field, and from an engine’s output to a display. What you now have is a framework. Depth comes from the literature and from data.

For the physics and the equation, Urick’s *Principles of Underwater Sound* remains the place most people start, and Ainslie’s *Principles of Sonar Performance Modelling* is the modern, rigorous treatment with careful definitions.

For propagation engines, *Computational Ocean Acoustics* by Jensen, Kuperman, Porter and Schmidt covers every method in unit 5.

For signal processing and detection, Abraham’s *Underwater Acoustic Signal Processing* is thorough and recent.

For bistatic geometry, Cox’s chapter *Fundamentals of Bistatic Active Sonar* is the classic.

For terminology, ISO 18405 settles arguments about definitions and references.

The Sources page lists these with the papers behind the newer material: multistatic tracking, continuous active sonar, bistatic target strength and learned surrogates.

Then go to the data. Take one real prediction from your own system and trace a single cell through every stage, as in unit 17. Nothing on this site will teach as much as that.

:::key
An expert in this field is someone who, shown a prediction, can say which term is driving it, which input that term depends on, how well that input is known, and what the display is hiding.
:::
