# Color and range

## Color maps

A display turns numbers into color. The mapping is a choice, and it changes what people see.

The eye judges **lightness** much more reliably than hue. A good map for a quantity that runs from low to high therefore gets steadily lighter, or steadily darker, from one end to the other. Viridis, magma, inferno and cividis were designed that way, and they stay readable for people with color vision deficiency and in grayscale.

A **rainbow** map does not. Its lightness rises to a bright yellow and cyan, then falls again. The eye reads those bright bands as edges. A smooth gradient in the data appears to have boundaries exactly where the color map has its bright stripes, and nowhere else.

::play

:::try
Compare display A and display B on the signal excess field. On the rainbow, count the apparent zones. On the uniform map, look for the same zones. Then read the lightness plot under the two images and match the humps in the rainbow’s curve to the bands you saw.
:::

:::key
A color map should add nothing the data does not contain. If a boundary appears in the picture, it should be because the numbers change there.
:::

## Choosing a map for the quantity

Different quantities call for different kinds of map.

**Sequential**, one direction, light to dark. For quantities with a natural low and high: transmission loss, probability of detection. Decide which end is bright and keep it consistent. For loss, bright usually means low loss, good propagation.

**Diverging**, two hues meeting at a neutral middle. For quantities with a meaningful zero: **signal excess** is the prime example. One hue for positive, another for negative, and a neutral tone at zero puts the 50 percent line exactly where the color changes character. The display workshop includes one. Choose it for signal excess with a range symmetric about zero, and notice that its lightness plot is a V: darkest at the middle, lighter toward both ends.

**Classed**, a few discrete colors. Green, yellow, red for good, marginal, poor. Easy to read at a glance and easy to misuse, as a later leaf explains.

Legacy systems and their users are often attached to the rainbow. Changing a familiar display has a real cost in training and trust. A reasonable path is to offer the better map as an option, show both side by side, and let the comparison make the argument.

:::operator
Whatever the map, the legend must be on the screen with numbers on it. A color with no scale is decoration.
:::

## Dynamic range and clipping

The range of values a color map spans is as important as the map itself.

Transmission loss in one field can run from 50 dB to more than 150 dB. A display must choose which part of that to spread its colors across.

- **Too wide**, and the interesting 20 dB near the detection threshold occupies a sliver of the scale. Everything looks the same.
- **Too narrow**, and everything outside is clipped to the end colors. A cell 5 dB too weak and a cell 50 dB too weak look identical.

Both hide information. A good default centers the range on the values that decide detection. For loss, that is around the allowable one-way loss from the figure of merit. For signal excess, it is around zero, perhaps −30 to +30 dB.

:::try
In the display workshop, narrow the color range on the signal excess field until both ends clip. The picture becomes high-contrast and decisive. Now widen it again and see how much gradation the narrow range was discarding.
:::

:::pitfall
Auto-scaling the color range to each new field makes every picture use the full palette, and makes two pictures impossible to compare. A calm day and a terrible day look equally colorful. Fix the range when comparison matters.
:::

## Thresholds, contours and classes

Sometimes a display should commit: here is where detection is likely, here is where it is not.

A **contour** at signal excess zero, or probability 50 percent, draws that line on top of the full field. The gradation stays visible beneath it. This is usually the best of both.

A **classed** display replaces the field with a few colors. It reads instantly. It also:

- **Hides everything inside a class.** Signal excess of +1 dB and +25 dB are both green.
- **Turns small differences into large ones at a boundary.** −0.1 dB is yellow and +0.1 dB is green, though no measurement could tell them apart.
- **Hides uncertainty.** A crisp green edge implies knowledge to within a cell.

:::operator
Remember what the zero contour means: even odds. Inside it, detection is more likely than not. Outside it, less. Treat the line as the middle of a blurry band several decibels wide, not as an edge.
:::

# Reading the products

## Reading a full-field

A full-field shows range across, depth down, for one bearing. Read it in a fixed order and it gives up its information quickly.

1. **Find the sensor.** Left edge, at its depth.
2. **Find the layer.** A horizontal change in character near the surface marks the mixed layer.
3. **Find the shadows.** Dark regions the sensor does not reach. Note their depth band and where they begin in range.
4. **Follow the bright paths.** A band hugging the surface is a duct. An arc going deep and returning is a convergence zone path. Diagonal stripes are bottom bounce.
5. **Read across at a target depth.** That row is the loss curve for that depth.
6. **Read down at a range.** That column says which depths are covered there.

The full-field is the display for depth questions: where could a target hide, and at what depth should the sensor be to deny it?

:::pitfall
A full-field is for **one** bearing. In water that changes with direction, the neighboring bearing may look different. Before generalizing from one, look at a few around the compass.
:::

## Reading a plan view

A plan view shows the area around the sensor from above, for **one target depth**.

1. **Check the target depth** in the caption. Everything shown is for that depth only.
2. **Check the assumptions:** target aspect, processor, modeled or measured noise.
3. **Find the direct path zone**, the filled region around the sensor.
4. **Look for rings** farther out: bottom bounce and convergence zones.
5. **Look for asymmetry.** A bite out of one side is a bearing where something differs: a shoal, a noisy beam, a baffle.
6. **Note the gaps** between zones. They are as much a result as the coverage.

The plan view is the display for position questions: where to put the next sensor, which way a target can approach unseen.

:::operator
A notch in one direction has a short list of causes. If it follows the bathymetry, it is propagation. If it points at a ship, it is noise. If it follows the platform’s stern, it is the array. If it points along one radial and nowhere else, suspect the data.
:::

## Signal excess or probability

Both can be drawn. They suit different readers.

**Signal excess** is in decibels. It is linear in the things an analyst can change: 3 dB more source level moves every noise-limited value by 3. It shows *how far* from threshold a cell is, in either direction, which is what you need to judge robustness.

**Probability of detection** runs from 0 to 1. It is what a planner wants. It compresses the extremes: +15 dB and +30 dB both show as nearly certain, and −15 dB and −30 dB both as nearly impossible.

That compression has consequences.

- Probability makes strong coverage look uniform, hiding how much margin there is.
- Probability depends on the fluctuation $\sigma$. A small $\sigma$ gives a sharp-edged map. A large one gives a soft map. Same physics, different picture.

:::key
Use signal excess to understand and diagnose. Use probability to plan and brief. Offer both, and show $\sigma$ wherever probability appears.
:::

## Curves and summary numbers

The oldest display is a line: signal excess against range for one bearing and one target depth. It remains the clearest way to compare alternatives. Two source depths, two processors, modeled against measured noise: overlaid as curves, the difference is plain in a way two color maps rarely manage.

From curves come summary numbers.

- **Detection range**: the outer edge of the first zone.
- **Detection zones**: the list of intervals with positive signal excess.
- **Best depth**: the sensor depth that maximizes coverage against a target depth band.

Summaries are needed, because nobody briefs a color map over a voice circuit. They also discard nearly everything.

:::pitfall
A single range hides the zones beyond the first, the dependence on target depth, the dependence on bearing and the uncertainty. When a summary number is produced, keep the path back to the field it came from one step away.
:::

# Honesty in display

## Smoothing and interpolation artifacts

Fields are computed on grids and drawn at screen resolution. What happens in between changes the picture.

**Smoothing** between cells makes a field look continuous. It also blurs real sharp edges such as shadow boundaries, and it can create values that exist nowhere in the data, halfway between a duct and a shadow.

**Nearest-neighbor** drawing keeps every cell honest and shows the grid, which is unattractive and truthful about resolution.

**Polar resampling** adds its own marks: wedges at long range where radials are far apart, a crowded center where they are dense, and faint spokes if neighboring radials disagree.

Turn off “Smooth between cells” in the display workshop to see the cells as they were computed.

:::pipeline
A display should not show finer detail than was computed. If the engine ran on 5° radials, a plan view that looks smooth at 0.5° is showing interpolation, and a zoom control should not pretend otherwise.
:::

## Modeled and measured side by side

Once measured beam noise and measured reverberation are available, a display has two versions of the truth. How they are shown matters.

- **Label the source of every term.** A caption such as “noise: measured 14:02, reverberation: modeled” removes a whole class of confusion.
- **Show age.** A measurement has a time. Past some age it should be flagged, then dropped in favor of the model.
- **Show the difference.** The most informative picture is often modeled minus measured. A flat offset says the model’s level is off. A pattern says its physics or its inputs are.
- **Do not blend silently.** If some beams use measured noise and others use the model because a measurement was missing, mark which.

:::operator
When modeled and measured disagree, the measurement is right about here and now. The disagreement is the valuable part. It says how much to trust the model for the places and times that have no measurement.
:::

## Showing uncertainty

A prediction field has no error bars. That absence is the largest misstatement a display makes, because it is made silently.

There are honest ways to show doubt.

- **Bands on curves.** Draw the median with a shaded band between, say, the 10th and 90th percentile.
- **Two contours.** Draw the 50 percent line and also the 20 and 80 percent lines. The gap between them is the uncertainty, made visible as distance.
- **Scenario pairs.** Show the field for the best and worst plausible case of the least certain input: beam and bow aspect, hard and soft bottom.
- **Fading.** Lower the saturation where the result depends heavily on poorly known inputs.

Any of these is better than a sharp edge.

:::key
The purpose is not to make the display look less sure of itself. It is to help the reader put their caution in the right place: on the flat parts of the curve, the far zones, and the assumptions about the target.
:::

## What operators ask of a display

A prediction display exists to support a few recurring decisions. Each one maps to a view.

| Question | View |
|---|---|
| What depth should my sensor be at? | Full-fields for several sensor depths, side by side |
| Where can a target hide from me? | Full-field shadows, plan view gaps |
| Which mode or processor should I use? | Signal excess curves per processor, overlaid |
| How far apart can I place sensors? | Plan view extent at the worst-case target depth |
| How much should I trust this? | Modeled against measured, and the spread |
| What changed since the last one? | Difference from the previous prediction |

A display that answers these directly is doing its job. One that offers every field at every setting, and leaves the operator to work out which matters, is a data browser.

:::operator
The best test of a display is to state the decision first, then see how many steps it takes to reach a picture that settles it.
:::
