# One ping

## What active sonar does

A passive sonar listens for sound a target makes. An active sonar makes its own sound, a **ping**, and listens for what comes back. That one difference shapes everything in this curriculum.

Active sonar exists because quiet targets leave nothing to listen for. A ping does not need the target to cooperate. It also gives **range** directly, because sound that left at a known time and returned later has travelled a known distance.

The price is paid in three ways:

- The ping travels **out and back**, so the ocean takes its toll twice.
- The ocean itself answers the ping. Sound scattered from the surface, the seabed and the water arrives along with the echo. That is **reverberation**.
- The ping announces where the source is to anyone listening.

:::key
Passive detection is a contest between target noise and background noise. Active detection is a contest between the **echo** and everything else that arrives with it: **reverberation** and **noise**.
:::

## The life of a ping

Follow one ping from start to finish. Every later unit zooms in on one of these steps.

1. **Transmit.** The source radiates a pulse at a known level, the *source level*, with a chosen waveform.
2. **Travel out.** The pulse spreads, bends with the sound speed profile, is absorbed and strikes the boundaries. What is lost is *transmission loss*.
3. **Meet the target.** The target returns a fraction of what reached it. That fraction is its *target strength*.
4. **Travel back.** The echo pays transmission loss again on the way to the receiver.
5. **Arrive in company.** The echo lands on the array together with reverberation from the same ping and with noise that was there all along.
6. **Beamform.** The array combines its hydrophones to favor one direction, which rejects noise from elsewhere.
7. **Process.** A processor matched to the waveform pulls the echo out of what masks it.
8. **Decide and display.** The output is compared to a threshold and shown to an operator.

::play

:::try
Slide the target out to 16 km and watch the echo sink into the noise floor. Bring it back to 4 km and lengthen the pulse: the echo is still there, but the reverberation under it has risen.
:::

## Echo, reverberation and noise

Three things reach the receiver. Tell them apart by what they depend on.

| | Comes from | Arrives | If you raise source level |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Echo** | The target | Once, at a time set by range | Rises |
| **Reverberation** | Scatterers in the sea, on the surface and the seabed | Continuously after the ping, decaying | Rises by the same amount |
| **Noise** | Wind, shipping, biology, your own platform | All the time, ping or no ping | Unchanged |

Two consequences follow, and they are worth committing to memory now.

- Against **noise**, a louder ping helps. The echo rises and the noise does not.
- Against **reverberation**, a louder ping does nothing. Echo and reverberation rise together.

So the first question to ask of any active prediction is which one is the limit at the range you care about. Early in the listening period it is usually reverberation. Late, after the reverberation has died away, it is noise.

:::operator
On a time trace, reverberation is the slope that falls away after the ping. Noise is the flat floor it lands on. An echo is a bump that stands above whichever of the two is higher at that instant.
:::

# The prediction

## What sensor performance prediction answers

Sensor performance prediction, SPP, answers one question before the ping is ever sent:

> Given this ocean, this sensor, this processor and this target, **where would I detect it, and how likely is that?**

The answer is a field, a value at every range, depth and bearing around the sensor. It is used to make decisions:

- At what depth should the sensor operate?
- Which waveform and processor should be used?
- How far apart can sensors be placed and still leave no gap?
- Where can a target hide, and how do I take that away?

A prediction is a forecast. Its inputs are estimates: the sound speed profile comes from a database or a forecast, the seabed from a chart of sediment provinces, the target from an assumption about depth and aspect. A good prediction carries its uncertainty with it, and a good display says which inputs were measured and which were assumed.

:::pipeline
In a working prediction system the pieces are separate services: environmental data, a propagation engine, a layer that turns loss into signal excess, and displays. This curriculum follows the data through all of them.
:::

## The sonar equation as a budget

The sonar equation is bookkeeping in decibels. Start with what was transmitted, subtract what was lost, add what the target returned, and compare the result with what masks it.

The echo that arrives:

$$
EL = SL - TL_{out} - TL_{back} + TS
$$

The margin by which that echo beats the masking and the detection threshold is the **signal excess**:

$$
SE = EL - \underbrace{(N \oplus RL)}_{\text{masking}} - DT
$$

The symbol $\oplus$ means a power sum, covered in the next unit. Each term has a unit of its own later on:

| Term | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $SL$ | Source level | 7, 12 |
| $TL$ | Transmission loss | 5, 6 |
| $TS$ | Target strength | 8 |
| $N$ | Noise at the beam | 7, 9 |
| $RL$ | Reverberation level | 10 |
| $DT$ | Detection threshold | 11, 12 |
| $SE$ | Signal excess | 13 |

:::key
Signal excess of zero means the echo is exactly at threshold, which by convention is a 50 percent chance of detection. Positive is better than even. Negative is worse.
:::

## From engine to pixels

A prediction flows through stages. Knowing the stages tells you where a wrong number came from.

1. **Environment.** Sound speed profiles, bathymetry, bottom type, wind and shipping, by position.
2. **Propagation engine.** Computes how sound travels from the sensor in each direction: transmission loss, and for ray engines the individual paths with their angles and times.
3. **Level terms.** Echo level from loss and target strength. Reverberation from loss and scattering. Noise from the environment and the array.
4. **Signal excess and probability.** The terms combined, per sensor and per processor.
5. **Display products.** A **full-field** shows range against depth along one bearing. A **plan view** shows bearing against range at one target depth.

Measured data can replace modeled terms on the way. A sonar that has measured the noise on its beams, or the reverberation after its last ping, knows those two terms better than any database.

:::pipeline
The full-field and the plan view are two slices of the same result set. If they disagree at the cell they share, the bug is in the slicing, not the physics.
:::

# Geometries and vocabulary

## Monostatic, bistatic, multistatic

The geometry names say where the source and the receiver are.

- **Monostatic.** Source and receiver in the same place, usually the same platform. Sound goes out and returns along the same path. Equal echo times fall on circles around the sensor.
- **Bistatic.** One source and one receiver, separated by a **baseline**. The outbound and return paths differ. Equal echo times fall on ellipses with the source and receiver at the foci.
- **Multistatic.** Several sources, several receivers or both. Every source pairs with every receiver, and each pair is a bistatic problem of its own. The art is in combining them.

Monostatic is where the fundamentals are learned, and it takes up most of this curriculum. Bistatic changes the geometry, the target strength and the timing. Multistatic adds planning and fusion.

:::operator
A field of sonobuoys with a few sources and many receivers is multistatic. So is a ship pinging while a helicopter’s dipping sonar and a towed array on another ship both listen.
:::

## Sensors, beams and processors

Three words are used precisely from here on.

A **sensor** is a physical array on a platform at a position and depth. It has a geometry, and therefore a set of **beams**: directions it can listen in, each with a width, and each with a vertical angle called **D/E**, for depression or elevation.

A **processor** is one way of treating the sensor’s data: a frequency band, a waveform, a filter matched to it, a detector and a display. A single sensor usually runs **several processors at once**. One may use a long tone to measure Doppler. Another may use a frequency sweep for fine range. They see the same water and the same target, and they do not get the same answer.

:::pipeline
Results are indexed by **sensor and processor**, not by sensor alone. Position, depth and array geometry belong to the sensor. Band, pulse, detection threshold, and with them noise, reverberation and even loss, belong to the processor.
:::

## Modeled and measured

Every term in the sonar equation can come from one of two places.

A **modeled** term is computed from a description of the world: a profile from climatology, a bottom type from a database, a wind speed from a forecast. It is available everywhere and in advance. It is only as good as that description.

A **measured** term is observed by the sensor itself. Two are common in active sonar:

- **Measured beam noise**: the noise level on each beam in each processor band, taken when no echo or reverberation is present.
- **Measured reverberation**: the level against time after a ping, on each beam, for each processor.

Measured terms are true for the moment and place they were taken. They do not extend to another depth, another waveform or tomorrow without help from a model.

:::key
Modeled data tells you what to expect. Measured data tells you what is. The strongest prediction uses measurements to correct the model, then uses the corrected model to reach where the measurements cannot.
:::
